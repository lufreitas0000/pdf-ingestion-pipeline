# Problem 3.11

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 11

## Problem Statement

11. Consider a particle of charge $e$ traveling in the electromagnetic potentials
    $$
    \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}, t) = -\nabla \lambda(\mathbf{r}, t), \quad \phi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \lambda(\mathbf{r}, t)}{\partial t}
    $$
    where $\lambda(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is an arbitrary scalar function.
    (a) What are the electromagnetic fields described by these potentials?
    (b) Show that the wave function of the particle is given by
    $$
    \psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp \left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \lambda(\mathbf{r}, t) \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t),
    $$
    where $\psi^{(0)}$ solves the Schrödinger equation with $\lambda \equiv 0$.
    (c) Let $v(\mathbf{r}, t) = e\phi(t)$ be a spatially uniform time varying potential. Show that
    $$
    \psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp \left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar} \int_{-\infty}^t \phi(t') \, \dd t' \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t).
    $$
    [Why is the lower limit on the integral $-\infty$?]

## Restatement

Consider a particle with charge $e$ moving in an electromagnetic field described by the scalar potential $\phi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ and the vector potential $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}, t)$. Suppose these potentials are given by:
$$
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}, t) = -\nabla \lambda(\mathbf{r}, t), \quad \phi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \lambda(\mathbf{r}, t)}{\partial t}
$$
where $\lambda(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is an arbitrary scalar function.

(a) Determine the physical electromagnetic fields $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ and $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ corresponding to these potentials.

(b) Show that if $\psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is a solution to the free-particle Schrödinger equation (where $\lambda = 0$), then the wave function
$$
\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp \left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \lambda(\mathbf{r}, t) \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t)
$$
is a solution to the Schrödinger equation with the potentials $\mathbf{A}$ and $\phi$.

(c) Given a spatially uniform, time-varying potential $v(\mathbf{r}, t) = e\phi(t)$, show that the wave function can be written as
$$
\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp \left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar} \int_{-\infty}^t \phi(t') \, \dd t' \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t).
$$
Explain why the lower limit of the integral is chosen to be $-\infty$.


## Prerequisites

- **Electromagnetic Fields**: The physical electric and magnetic fields are derived from the scalar and vector potentials via $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}$.
- **Schrödinger Equation in EM Fields**: The Hamiltonian for a charged particle in an electromagnetic field is $\hat{H} = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}\right)^2 + e\phi$, with $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar\nabla$.


## Hints

> **Hint 1** — For part (a), directly apply the definitions of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ in terms of the potentials $\mathbf{A}$ and $\phi$. Remember that the curl of a gradient is identically zero.
> **Hint 2** — For part (b), evaluate the action of the kinematic momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}$ on the given wave function $\psi$. Apply the product rule carefully.
> **Hint 3** — Notice how the spatial derivative of the exponential phase perfectly cancels the vector potential term, leaving a clean factor to evaluate $(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A})^2 \psi$.
> **Hint 4** — For part (c), construct the corresponding $\lambda(t)$ that produces $\phi(t)$ and $\mathbf{A}=0$. Consider the boundary condition on the state at $t \to -\infty$ to justify the lower limit of integration.


## Solution

### Step 1: Evaluating the Electromagnetic Fields

The physical electromagnetic fields are determined by the scalar potential $\phi$ and vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ via the relations (in Gaussian units):
$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = -\nabla \phi(\mathbf{r}, t) - \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}, t)}{\partial t}
$$
$$
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}, t)
$$
Substituting the given potentials $\mathbf{A} = -\nabla \lambda$ and $\phi = \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t}$:
$$
\mathbf{E} = -\nabla \left( \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t} \right) - \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial (-\nabla \lambda)}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{c} \nabla \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{c} \nabla \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t} = 0
$$
$$
\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times (-\nabla \lambda) = 0
$$
Since the curl of any gradient is exactly zero, both $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ vanish identically everywhere.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Schrödinger Equation Under Gauge Transformation

We must show that $\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda(\mathbf{r}, t)\right) \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ satisfies the Schrödinger equation with potentials $\mathbf{A}$ and $\phi$, assuming $\psi^{(0)}$ is a solution to the field-free equation:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi^{(0)}}{\partial t} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \psi^{(0)}
$$
The full Schrödinger equation we want to verify is:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \left[ \frac{1}{2m} \left( \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \right)^2 + e\phi \right] \psi
$$
Let us evaluate the left-hand side (LHS) by differentiating $\psi$ with respect to time:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left[ \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \psi^{(0)} \right]
$$
$$
= i\hbar \left( -\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t} \right) \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \psi^{(0)} + \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi^{(0)}}{\partial t}
$$
Using the given potential $\phi = \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t}$, the first term simplifies directly to $e\phi \psi$:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = e\phi \psi + \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi^{(0)}}{\partial t}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Action of the Kinematic Momentum Operator

Next, we evaluate the action of the kinematic momentum operator $\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} = -i\hbar\nabla - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}$ on $\psi$. Applying the product rule:
$$
\left( -i\hbar\nabla - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \right) \psi = -i\hbar\nabla \left[ \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \psi^{(0)} \right] - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \psi
$$
$$
= -i\hbar \left[ \left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\nabla \lambda\right) \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \psi^{(0)} + \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \nabla \psi^{(0)} \right] - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \psi
$$
$$
= -\frac{e}{c}(\nabla \lambda) \psi + \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) (-i\hbar\nabla) \psi^{(0)} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \psi
$$
Since the vector potential is $\mathbf{A} = -\nabla \lambda$, the first and last terms perfectly cancel:
$$
\left( \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \right) \psi = \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \hat{\mathbf{p}} \psi^{(0)}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Completing the Verification

To find the action of the squared operator, we apply $\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A}$ a second time. We can reuse the result of Step 3 by treating $\hat{\mathbf{p}} \psi^{(0)}$ as our new target state:
$$
\left( \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \right)^2 \psi = \left( \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \right) \left[ \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \hat{\mathbf{p}} \psi^{(0)} \right] = \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \hat{\mathbf{p}}^2 \psi^{(0)}
$$
Substituting this into the right-hand side (RHS) of the full Hamiltonian yields:
$$
\left[ \frac{1}{2m} \left( \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \frac{e}{c}\mathbf{A} \right)^2 + e\phi \right] \psi = \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \psi^{(0)} + e\phi \psi
$$
Equating the evaluated LHS (Step 2) with this RHS:
$$
e\phi \psi + \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi^{(0)}}{\partial t} = \exp\left(-\frac{ie}{\hbar c}\lambda\right) \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \psi^{(0)} + e\phi \psi
$$
Subtracting $e\phi \psi$ from both sides and canceling the overall phase factor gives:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi^{(0)}}{\partial t} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \psi^{(0)}
$$
This is exactly the assumed field-free equation for $\psi^{(0)}$, completing the proof.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 5: Spatially Uniform Time-Varying Potential

For part (c), we have a spatially uniform potential $v(\mathbf{r}, t) = e\phi(t)$. This implies $\phi$ only depends on $t$, and the vector potential is $\mathbf{A} = 0$.
We can express this via the gauge framework of parts (a) and (b) by identifying a $\lambda(\mathbf{r}, t)$ such that:
$$
\mathbf{A} = -\nabla \lambda = 0 \implies \lambda = \lambda(t)
$$
$$
\phi(t) = \frac{1}{c} \frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} \implies \lambda(t) = c \int_{t_0}^t \phi(t') \, \dd t'
$$
Substituting this $\lambda(t)$ into the general wave function phase formula from part (b):
$$
\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp\left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar c} \left( c \int_{t_0}^t \phi(t') \, \dd t' \right) \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp\left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar} \int_{t_0}^t \phi(t') \, \dd t' \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t)
$$
The choice of the lower integration limit $t_0 = -\infty$ enforces the initial condition $\lambda(-\infty) = 0$. Physically, this choice assumes the perturbation $\phi(t)$ was absent in the distant past ($t \to -\infty$), ensuring the state asymptotically approaches the unperturbed free-particle state $\psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t \to -\infty)$. Any other lower limit $t_0$ would merely multiply the state by a time-independent global phase factor, leaving all physical observables completely unaffected.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \exp \left[ -\frac{ie}{\hbar} \int_{-\infty}^t \phi(t') \, \dd t' \right] \psi^{(0)}(\mathbf{r}, t)}
$$


## Discussion

This problem highlights the quantum mechanical counterpart of classical gauge invariance. In classical electromagnetism, shifting the potentials by $\mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{A} - \nabla \lambda$ and $\phi \to \phi + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial t}$ leaves the observable $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ fields unchanged. In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ depends explicitly on the potentials. To ensure that probabilities and observable quantities like position and momentum expectation values remain invariant under this gauge transformation, the wave function must absorb a local coordinate- and time-dependent phase shift $\exp(-ie\lambda/\hbar c)$. Part (c) demonstrates that applying a spatially uniform but time-varying potential simply advances the quantum mechanical phase of the state according to the integrated energy shift.
