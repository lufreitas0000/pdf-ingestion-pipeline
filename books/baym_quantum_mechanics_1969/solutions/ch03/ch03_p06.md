# Problem 3.6

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 6

## Problem Statement

6. What is the expectation value of the kinetic energy of a particle in terms of its wave function?

---

## Restatement

Given a particle whose state is described by a normalized wave function $\psi(\mathbf{r})$, find the expectation value of its kinetic energy $\langle \hat{T} \rangle$ expressed as an integral over the spatial coordinates.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Kinetic Energy Operator**: The operator for the kinetic energy of a particle is given by $\hat{T} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2$.
- **Expectation Value**: The expectation value of an observable represented by an operator $\hat{A}$ in a state $\ket{\Psi}$ is $\langle \hat{A} \rangle = \bra{\Psi} \hat{A} \ket{\Psi}$.
- **Integration by Parts**: In three dimensions, integration by parts can be used to shift derivatives from one function to another, utilizing the divergence theorem and assuming the wave function vanishes at spatial infinity.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Start by writing the abstract expectation value $\bra{\Psi} \hat{T} \ket{\Psi}$ for the kinetic energy.
> **Hint 2** — Insert the position basis completeness relation $\int \dd^3 r \, \ket{\mathbf{r}} \bra{\mathbf{r}} = \hat{1}$ to convert the abstract bra-ket notation into an integral involving the wave function $\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi}$.
> **Hint 3** — Remember that the momentum operator in the position representation is $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar\nabla$. Apply this to find how $\hat{T}$ acts on the wave function.
> **Hint 4** — Once you have the expression involving the Laplacian $\nabla^2$, try using integration by parts (or Green's first identity) to rewrite the integrand in a manifestly positive-definite form.

---

## Solution

### Setup

Let the state of the particle be $\ket{\Psi}$, and let its normalized wave function in the position representation be $\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi}$. The kinetic energy operator is $\hat{T} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m}$. We want to evaluate the expectation value $\langle \hat{T} \rangle = \bra{\Psi} \hat{T} \ket{\Psi}$.

### Step 1: Kinetic Energy Operator in Position Basis

We evaluate the expectation value by inserting a complete set of position states:
$$
\langle \hat{T} \rangle = \bra{\Psi} \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \ket{\Psi} = \int \dd^3 r \, \braket{\Psi}{\mathbf{r}} \bra{\mathbf{r}} \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \ket{\Psi}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Action of the Momentum Operator

In the position representation, the momentum operator is $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar\nabla$. Therefore, the kinetic energy operator acts on the wave function as follows:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{r}} \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \ket{\Psi} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Integral Expression

Substituting this back into the expectation value integral, we obtain the first form of the kinetic energy expectation value:
$$
\langle \hat{T} \rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \int \dd^3 r \, \psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla^2 \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Alternative Positive-Definite Form

We can rewrite this expression into a more symmetric and manifestly positive form by integrating by parts. Using the vector calculus identity $\nabla \cdot (\psi^* \nabla \psi) = \nabla \psi^* \cdot \nabla \psi + \psi^* \nabla^2 \psi$, we have:
$$
\psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla^2 \psi(\mathbf{r}) = \nabla \cdot (\psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})) - \nabla \psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \cdot \nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})
$$
Integrating this over all space:
$$
\int \dd^3 r \, \psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla^2 \psi(\mathbf{r}) = \int \dd^3 r \, \nabla \cdot (\psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})) - \int \dd^3 r \, |\nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})|^2
$$
By the divergence theorem, the first integral on the right-hand side can be converted to a surface integral at infinity. Assuming the wave function represents a bound state or a normalizable wave packet, $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ and its gradient vanish at infinity, making the surface integral zero. Thus:
$$
\int \dd^3 r \, \psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla^2 \psi(\mathbf{r}) = - \int \dd^3 r \, |\nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})|^2
$$
Substituting this back yields the alternative form:
$$
\langle \hat{T} \rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \int \dd^3 r \, |\nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})|^2
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{ \langle \hat{T} \rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \int \dd^3 r \, \psi^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla^2 \psi(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \int \dd^3 r \, |\nabla \psi(\mathbf{r})|^2 }
$$

---

## Discussion

The expectation value of the kinetic energy can be expressed in two equivalent ways. The first form, involving the Laplacian $\nabla^2$, directly uses the canonical definition of the kinetic energy operator in the position basis. The second form, $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \int |\nabla \psi|^2 \, \dd^3 r$, is manifestly real and non-negative, which correctly reflects the physical property that kinetic energy cannot be negative. This second form also clearly demonstrates that higher kinetic energy is associated with a more rapidly varying wave function (larger gradients).
