# Problem 3.3

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 3

## Problem Statement

3. What is the representation of the position operator in the momentum basis, i.e., how is $\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi}$ related to $\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi}$?

---

## Restatement

Find the action of the position operator $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ on an arbitrary state $\ket{\Psi}$ in the momentum representation. In other words, express the matrix element $\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi}$ strictly in terms of the momentum-space wave function $\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi}$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Position operator in real space**: The position operator acting on a state in the position basis is equivalent to multiplication by the coordinate vector:
  $$ \bra{\mathbf{r}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = \mathbf{r} \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi} $$
- **Completeness of position states**: The continuous set of position states forms a complete basis:
  $$ \int \dd^3 r\, \ket{\mathbf{r}} \bra{\mathbf{r}} = 1 $$
- **Momentum eigenstates**: The projection of a momentum eigenstate onto a position eigenstate is given by the plane wave:
  $$ \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{p}} = e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar} \implies \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} = e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar} $$

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Insert a complete set of position states $\ket{\mathbf{r}}$ between the momentum bra $\bra{\mathbf{p}}$ and the position operator $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$.

> **Hint 2** — Apply the known action of the position operator on the position basis, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}\ket{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}\ket{\mathbf{r}}$.

> **Hint 3** — Use the explicit exponential form of the inner product $\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}}$ and recognize that multiplying by $\mathbf{r}$ is algebraically equivalent to taking a gradient with respect to $\mathbf{p}$.

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Insert a complete set of position states
To find the representation of $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ in the momentum basis, we start by evaluating the matrix element $\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi}$. We insert the completeness relation for position eigenstates, $\int \dd^3 r\, \ket{\mathbf{r}}\bra{\mathbf{r}} = 1$:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = \int \dd^3 r\, \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} \bra{\mathbf{r}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Apply the position operator in coordinate representation
The position operator acting on the state in the position representation is simply multiplication by the coordinate vector $\mathbf{r}$:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{r}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = \mathbf{r} \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi}
$$
Substituting this back into the integral yields:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = \int \dd^3 r\, \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} \mathbf{r} \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Express coordinate multiplication as a momentum gradient
We know that the position representation of a momentum eigenstate is given by the plane wave:
$$
\braket{\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{p}} = e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar}
$$
Taking the complex conjugate, we have the dual inner product:
$$
\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} = e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar}
$$
Notice that taking the gradient with respect to the momentum vector $\mathbf{p}$ of this inner product yields:
$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} = \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \left( e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar} \right) = \left( -\frac{i\mathbf{r}}{\hbar} \right) e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar} = -\frac{i}{\hbar} \mathbf{r} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}}
$$
Rearranging this to isolate $\mathbf{r} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}}$, we find:
$$
\mathbf{r} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} = i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Complete the integral
Substituting this resulting derivative form back into our full integral expression:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} &= \int \dd^3 r\, \left( i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} \right) \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi} \\
&= i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \int \dd^3 r\, \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{r}} \braket{\mathbf{r}}{\Psi}
\end{aligned}
$$
Recognizing that the integral on the right is exactly the expansion of $\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi}$ via the position completeness relation, we arrive at the final result:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{ \bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi} }
$$

---

## Discussion

This result establishes the fundamental canonical duality between the position and momentum representations in quantum mechanics. Just as the momentum operator acts as a spatial derivative in the position representation ($\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$), the position operator acts as a momentum derivative in the momentum representation ($\hat{\mathbf{r}} = i\hbar\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}$). The difference in sign originates directly from the choice of signs in the Fourier transform kernel $\braket{\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{p}} = e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar}$, which is deeply tied to the canonical commutation relation $\comm{\hat{x}_j}{\hat{p}_k} = i\hbar\delta_{jk}$. Both representations are entirely physically equivalent and merely represent different choices of basis for the underlying abstract Hilbert space.
