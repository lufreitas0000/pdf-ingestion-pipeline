# Problem 3.12

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 12

## Problem Statement

12. Consider doing a "two-slit interference" experiment where the slits are replaced by long conducting tubes. The source S emits particles in reasonably well defined wave packets, so that one can be sure [if the tubes are long enough] that for a certain time interval, say $t_0$ to $t_1$ seconds after emission, the wave packet of the particle is definitely within the tubes. During this time interval, a constant voltage $V_A$ is applied to tube A and a constant voltage $V_B$ is applied to tube B. The rest of the time there is no voltage on the tubes. These voltages produce no fields well within the tubes.
    (a) Describe how the interference pattern on the screen depends on $V_A$ and $V_B$.
    (b) If the phase difference between the amplitudes arriving at the screen from the two tubes is $Kz$, where $K = 10^4\text{ cm}^{-1}$, by how many cm is the diffraction pattern shifted if $V_A = 0$, $V_B = 10^{-6}\text{ volts}$, $t_1 - t_0 = 10^{-9}\text{ sec}$?

---

## Restatement

A two-slit interference experiment is performed using electrons (or particles of charge $q = -e$) traveling through two long conducting tubes, A and B. After the wave packets are fully inside the tubes, a constant electrical potential $V_A$ is applied to tube A and $V_B$ to tube B for a time duration $\tau = t_1 - t_0$. Because the tubes are conducting and long, the particles experience no electric field, only a constant scalar potential. 

In part (a), determine the dependence of the resulting interference pattern on the screen on the voltages $V_A$ and $V_B$.
In part (b), assume the purely geometric phase difference at a position $z$ on the screen is given by $\Delta \phi_{\text{geom}} = Kz$, with $K = 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-1}$. Calculate the spatial shift of the interference pattern in cm if $V_A = 0$, $V_B = 10^{-6} \text{ V}$, and $\tau = 10^{-9} \text{ s}$. Assuming the particles are electrons.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Scalar Potential Phase Shift**: The presence of a scalar potential $V(\mathbf{r}, t)$ modifies the quantum mechanical phase of a particle's wave function. For a potential $V$ applied over a time $\tau$, the accumulated extra phase is $\Delta \phi = -\frac{q}{\hbar} \int V \, \dd t = -\frac{q V \tau}{\hbar}$.
- **Interference Condition**: The observable interference pattern depends only on the relative phase difference between the two possible paths (through tube A and tube B).

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — How does the Hamiltonian of the particle change when a constant voltage is applied to the tube? Recall that inside a conductor, the potential is constant, so there is no force, but the scalar potential term $qV$ is added to the Hamiltonian.

> **Hint 2** — Integrate the time-dependent Schrödinger equation $i\hbar \frac{\partial \ket{\psi}}{\partial t} = (\hat{H}_0 + qV)\ket{\psi}$ to find the extra phase factor acquired by the wave packet traversing each tube.

> **Hint 3** — Calculate the new total phase difference at the screen by adding the geometric phase difference $Kz$ and the relative phase shift induced by the potentials. Set this total phase to a constant (e.g., $0$ for the central maximum) to find the new position $z$.

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Extra Phase due to Scalar Potential

When the wave packet is entirely inside a conducting tube, it is in a region of constant electrical potential. The potential energy of a particle with charge $q$ inside a tube with voltage $V$ is $U = qV$. The Schrödinger equation in the region of the tube becomes:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial \ket{\psi}}{\partial t} = (\hat{H}_0 + qV) \ket{\psi}
$$
where $\hat{H}_0$ is the Hamiltonian in the absence of the applied voltage. Since the potential is spatially uniform inside the tube, it commutes with $\hat{H}_0$. The state vector acquires an overall time-dependent phase factor:
$$
\ket{\psi(t)} = \exp\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar} qV t\right) \ket{\psi_0(t)}
$$
where $\ket{\psi_0(t)}$ is the state vector in the absence of the applied voltage. The extra phase accumulated during the time interval $\tau = t_1 - t_0$ is:
$$
\phi = -\frac{q V \tau}{\hbar}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Interference Pattern Dependence (Part a)

The amplitude of the wave arriving at a point on the screen is the superposition of the amplitudes from path A and path B:
$$
\psi_{\text{screen}} = \psi_A + \psi_B = |\psi_A| e^{i \phi_{A,\text{geom}}} e^{i \phi_A} + |\psi_B| e^{i \phi_{B,\text{geom}}} e^{i \phi_B}
$$
The relative phase difference between the two paths is:
$$
\Delta \Phi = (\phi_{A,\text{geom}} + \phi_A) - (\phi_{B,\text{geom}} + \phi_B) = \Delta \phi_{\text{geom}} - \frac{q (V_A - V_B) \tau}{\hbar}
$$
The probability density on the screen is proportional to $|\psi_A + \psi_B|^2$, which contains an interference term $\cos(\Delta \Phi)$. Thus, the interference pattern is shifted by the phase difference:
$$
\Delta \phi_{\text{shift}} = -\frac{q (V_A - V_B) \tau}{\hbar}
$$
The pattern depends only on the potential difference $(V_A - V_B)$, not on the absolute potentials, and shifts the entire interference fringes uniformly.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Spatial Shift Calculation (Part b)

Given the geometric phase difference is $\Delta \phi_{\text{geom}} = Kz$, the total phase difference is:
$$
\Delta \Phi = Kz - \frac{q (V_A - V_B) \tau}{\hbar}
$$
The central maximum of the interference pattern originally located at $z_0 = 0$ (where $\Delta \Phi = 0$) will be shifted to a new position $z$ such that:
$$
Kz - \frac{q (V_A - V_B) \tau}{\hbar} = 0 \implies z = \frac{q (V_A - V_B) \tau}{\hbar K}
$$
Assuming the particles emitted by the source are electrons, we have $q = -e$. The voltages are $V_A = 0$ and $V_B = 10^{-6} \text{ V}$, so $(V_A - V_B) = -10^{-6} \text{ V}$. 
The time interval is $\tau = 10^{-9} \text{ s}$. 
Substituting these values:
$$
z = \frac{(-e) (-10^{-6} \text{ V}) (10^{-9} \text{ s})}{\hbar K} = \frac{e \times 10^{-15} \text{ V s}}{\hbar K}
$$
Note that $e \times 1 \text{ V} = 1 \text{ eV}$. We can use $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16} \text{ eV s}$ and $K = 10^4 \text{ cm}^{-1}$:
$$
z = \frac{10^{-15} \text{ eV s}}{(6.582 \times 10^{-16} \text{ eV s})(10^4 \text{ cm}^{-1})} = \frac{10}{6.582 \times 10^4} \text{ cm} \approx 1.519 \times 10^{-4} \text{ cm}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{z \approx 1.52 \times 10^{-4} \text{ cm}}
$$

## Discussion

This problem is a scalar analogue of the Aharonov-Bohm effect. In the traditional Aharonov-Bohm effect, a phase shift is induced by a magnetic vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ even when the magnetic field $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A} = 0$ along the particle trajectories. Here, the phase shift is induced by an electric scalar potential $V$ even when the electric field $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V = 0$ inside the conducting tubes. Both effects demonstrate that electromagnetic potentials are not merely mathematical conveniences in quantum mechanics, but possess direct physical significance and can lead to measurable interference shifts.

## Notes

The assumption that the particles are electrons ($q = -e$) is necessary to obtain the numerical result. Other charged particles would yield a shift inversely proportional to their charge.
