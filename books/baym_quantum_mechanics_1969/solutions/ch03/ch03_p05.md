# Problem 3.5

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 5

## Problem Statement

5. What is $\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x}$? Compare these commutation relations for position and momentum with the classical Poisson bracket relations satisfied by $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{r}$.

---

## Restatement

Evaluate the commutator between the $y$-component of the position operator, $\hat{y}$, and the $x$-component of the momentum operator, $\hat{p}_x$. Generalize this to $\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j}$ and compare the resulting quantum commutation relations with the classical Poisson brackets $\{r_i, p_j\}$ for the canonical variables position $\mathbf{r}$ and momentum $\mathbf{p}$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Position Operator**: The operator corresponding to position, whose action in the position representation is multiplication by the coordinate, $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}$.
- **Momentum Operator**: The operator corresponding to momentum, whose action in the position representation is the gradient, $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar \nabla$.
- **Commutator**: The commutator of two operators $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ is defined as $\comm{\hat{A}}{\hat{B}} = \hat{A}\hat{B} - \hat{B}\hat{A}$.
- **Poisson Bracket**: The classical Poisson bracket of two phase space functions $f(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{p})$ and $g(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{p})$ is $\{f, g\} = \sum_k \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial r_k} \frac{\partial g}{\partial p_k} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_k} \frac{\partial g}{\partial r_k} \right)$.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Consider the action of the commutator $\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x}$ on an arbitrary smooth test wave function $\psi(x, y, z)$.

> **Hint 2** — Recall that in the position representation, $\hat{y}$ acts as multiplication by $y$, and $\hat{p}_x$ acts as the differential operator $-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$.

> **Hint 3** — Calculate the classical Poisson bracket $\{y, p_x\}$ using the standard definition in terms of phase space coordinates. Does the general result match the rule $\comm{\hat{f}}{\hat{g}} = i\hbar \{f, g\}_{\text{classical}}$?

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Evaluate the Quantum Commutator
In the position representation, the position operator $\hat{y}$ acts by multiplying by $y$, and the momentum operator $\hat{p}_x$ acts as $-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$. Let's apply the commutator to an arbitrary test function $\psi(\mathbf{r})$:
$$
\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x} \psi = (\hat{y} \hat{p}_x - \hat{p}_x \hat{y}) \psi
$$
Substituting the explicit differential forms for the operators:
$$
\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x} \psi = y \left(-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right) \psi - \left(-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right) (y \psi)
$$
Since $y$ and $x$ are independent coordinates, the partial derivative with respect to $x$ does not affect $y$. We can pull $y$ out of the derivative in the second term:
$$
\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x} \psi = -i\hbar y \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} - \left( -i\hbar y \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} \right) = 0
$$
Because this holds for any arbitrary wave function $\psi$, we conclude that the commutator itself is zero:
$$
\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x} = 0
$$

### Step 2: General Commutation Relations
We can generalize this to any components $\hat{r}_i$ and $\hat{p}_j$ (where $i, j \in \{x, y, z\}$):
$$
\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} \psi = r_i \left(-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial r_j}\right) \psi - \left(-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial r_j}\right) (r_i \psi)
$$
Using the product rule on the second term:
$$
\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} \psi = -i\hbar r_i \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r_j} + i\hbar \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial r_j} \psi + i\hbar r_i \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r_j}
$$
The first and third terms cancel out. Since $r_i$ and $r_j$ are independent Cartesian coordinates, $\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial r_j} = \delta_{ij}$:
$$
\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} \psi = i\hbar \delta_{ij} \psi
$$
Thus, the general quantum commutation relation is:
$$
\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} = i\hbar \delta_{ij}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Evaluate the Classical Poisson Bracket
The classical Poisson bracket between two observables $f(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{p})$ and $g(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{p})$ is defined as:
$$
\{f, g\} = \sum_{k \in \{x,y,z\}} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial r_k} \frac{\partial g}{\partial p_k} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_k} \frac{\partial g}{\partial r_k} \right)
$$
For $f = y$ and $g = p_x$, the relevant derivatives are $\frac{\partial y}{\partial y} = 1$ and $\frac{\partial p_x}{\partial p_x} = 1$. The sum evaluates to:
$$
\{y, p_x\} = \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} \frac{\partial p_x}{\partial p_y} - \dots = 1 \cdot 0 - 0 = 0
$$
More generally, for $f = r_i$ and $g = p_j$:
$$
\{r_i, p_j\} = \sum_k \left( \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial r_k} \frac{\partial p_j}{\partial p_k} - \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial p_k} \frac{\partial p_j}{\partial r_k} \right) = \sum_k \left( \delta_{ik} \delta_{jk} - 0 \cdot 0 \right) = \delta_{ij}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Compare Quantum and Classical Relations
Comparing the quantum result $\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} = i\hbar \delta_{ij}$ with the classical result $\{r_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$, we see they are related by:
$$
\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} = i\hbar \{r_i, p_j\}
$$
This represents Dirac's canonical quantization rule.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x} = 0}
$$
The commutation relations $\comm{\hat{r}_i}{\hat{p}_j} = i\hbar \delta_{ij}$ are exactly $i\hbar$ times the corresponding classical Poisson bracket relations $\{r_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$.

## Discussion

This problem illustrates the fundamental postulate of canonical quantization due to Dirac. In classical mechanics, position and momentum are independent canonical variables. The transition to quantum mechanics replaces these conjugate variables with linear operators and replaces the classical Poisson bracket $\{A, B\}$ with the operator commutator divided by $i\hbar$, i.e., $\frac{1}{i\hbar}\comm{\hat{A}}{\hat{B}}$. 

The specific finding that $\comm{\hat{y}}{\hat{p}_x} = 0$ means that measurements of the $y$-position and $x$-momentum are physically compatible. Unlike $x$ and $p_x$, which are subject to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle $\Delta x \Delta p_x \ge \hbar/2$, one can simultaneously prepare a quantum state with arbitrarily precise values of $y$ and $p_x$.
