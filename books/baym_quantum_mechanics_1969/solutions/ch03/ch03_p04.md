# Problem 3.4

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 4

## Problem Statement

4. Suppose that the potential is $v(\mathbf{r}) = (k/2)r^2$. What is the Schrödinger equation written in momentum space; that is, what is the equation of motion of the amplitude $\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}$?

---

## Restatement

Consider a particle moving in a three-dimensional harmonic oscillator potential $v(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{k}{2}r^2$, where $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector and $r = |\mathbf{r}|$. Find the Schrödinger equation in momentum space, which describes the time evolution of the momentum space probability amplitude $\Psi(\mathbf{p}, t) \equiv \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Schrödinger Equation**: The time evolution of a state vector $\ket{\Psi(t)}$ is governed by the Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} + v(\hat{\mathbf{r}})$ as $i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat{H}\ket{\Psi(t)}$.
- **Momentum Space Representation of Momentum**: In the momentum basis, the momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ acts multiplicatively: $\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{p}} \ket{\Psi} = \mathbf{p} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi}$.
- **Momentum Space Representation of Position**: In the momentum basis, the position operator $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ acts as a gradient with respect to momentum: $\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi} = i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi}$.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Start with the abstract Schrödinger equation $i\hbar \partial_t \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat{H}\ket{\Psi(t)}$ and project it onto the momentum basis by multiplying by the bra $\bra{\mathbf{p}}$.

> **Hint 2** — Express the Hamiltonian operator $\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} + \frac{k}{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}^2$ in terms of its action on the momentum amplitude $\braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}$.

> **Hint 3** — Remember from Problem 3.3 that the position operator in momentum space becomes $i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}$. Apply this operator twice to represent the $\hat{\mathbf{r}}^2$ term in the potential.

---

## Solution

### Setup

We start with the abstract time-dependent Schrödinger equation for a state vector $\ket{\Psi(t)}$:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat{H} \ket{\Psi(t)}
$$
where the Hamiltonian operator for a particle in the harmonic potential $v(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{k}{2}r^2$ is
$$
\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} + \frac{k}{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}^2
$$
To find the equation of motion in momentum space, we project the Schrödinger equation onto a momentum eigenstate $\bra{\mathbf{p}}$:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)} = \bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{H} \ket{\Psi(t)} = \bra{\mathbf{p}} \left( \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} + \frac{k}{2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}^2 \right) \ket{\Psi(t)}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 1: Evaluate the kinetic energy term

The momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ is diagonal in the momentum basis, meaning its action yields the eigenvalue $\mathbf{p}$:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^2}{2m} \ket{\Psi(t)} = \frac{p^2}{2m} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}
$$
where $p^2 = \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{p}$.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Evaluate the potential energy term

The position operator $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ acting in the momentum representation takes the form of a gradient with respect to momentum:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \ket{\Psi(t)} = i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}
$$
The potential energy depends on $\hat{\mathbf{r}}^2 = \hat{\mathbf{r}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}$. We apply the position operator representation twice:
$$
\bra{\mathbf{p}} \hat{\mathbf{r}}^2 \ket{\Psi(t)} = (i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}) \cdot (i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}) \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)} = -\hbar^2 \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}^2 \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}
$$
where $\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}^2 = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial p_x^2} + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial p_y^2} + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial p_z^2}$ is the Laplacian in momentum space.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Assemble the momentum space Schrödinger equation

Substitute the results from Step 1 and Step 2 back into the projected Schrödinger equation:
$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)} = \left( \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{k\hbar^2}{2} \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}^2 \right) \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}
$$
Writing $\Psi(\mathbf{p}, t) \equiv \braket{\mathbf{p}}{\Psi(t)}$ for the momentum space probability amplitude, we obtain the final equation.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \Psi(\mathbf{p}, t) = \left( -\frac{k\hbar^2}{2} \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}^2 + \frac{p^2}{2m} \right) \Psi(\mathbf{p}, t)
}
$$

---

## Discussion

The Schrödinger equation in momentum space for the harmonic oscillator has exactly the same mathematical structure as it does in position space. The roles of the coordinates and their derivatives are simply swapped: the kinetic energy term acts multiplicatively, while the potential energy term acts as a second derivative. This deep symmetry between position and momentum is unique to the harmonic oscillator and explains why its wavefunctions have identical functional forms (Gaussian times Hermite polynomials) in both real and momentum space.

## Related Resources

- **Typical method used**: Applying the abstract operator representation in the momentum basis, utilizing $\hat{\mathbf{r}} \to i\hbar \nabla_{\mathbf{p}}$.
- **Alternative approaches**: Taking the Fourier transform of the position-space Schrödinger equation. By substituting $\Psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \int \frac{\dd^3 p}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3/2}} e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}/\hbar} \Psi(\mathbf{p}, t)$ into the position-space equation and using integration by parts, one arrives at the same momentum-space equation.
- **References**: Griffiths, *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics* (Section on momentum space); Sakurai, *Modern Quantum Mechanics* (Chapter 1, momentum basis).
