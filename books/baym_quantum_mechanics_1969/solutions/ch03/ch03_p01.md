# Problem 3.1

**Source**: Chapter 3, Problem 1

## Problem Statement

1. In the "discrete" Schrödinger equation (3-12) for a free particle, $w_{i, i-1} = w_{i, i+1} \equiv w$, and $v_i = 0$. Assume *periodic boundary conditions*, that is, $\psi_{N+1}(t) = \psi_1(t)$, and generally $\psi_{j+N}(t) = \psi_j(t)$, where $N$ is a large integer.
   (a) Show that there are $N$ linearly independent solutions of this Schrödinger equation.
   (b) Find the $N$ normalized energy eigenstates, that is, those solutions that vary in time with a fixed frequency. What are the possible energy values for the particle? Show that these go over into the free particle energies, $p^2/2m$, in the continuum limit, i.e., as $\lambda$, the size of the intervals $\to 0$, but $N\lambda$ remains fixed.
   (c) We can define the *propagator matrix*, $K$, by $\psi_j(t) = \sum_k K_{jk}(t, t') \psi_k(t')$ in analogy with (3-91). Write down an explicit expression for the matrix elements $K_{jk}(t, t')$.

---

## Restatement

Consider the discrete Schrödinger equation for a free particle on a one-dimensional lattice with $N$ sites, uniform hopping frequency $w$, and zero potential ($v_i = 0$). The time-dependent probability amplitudes $\psi_j(t)$ at site $j$ obey:
$$ i \frac{d\psi_j(t)}{dt} = w [\psi_{j+1}(t) - 2\psi_j(t) + \psi_{j-1}(t)] $$
subject to periodic boundary conditions $\psi_{j+N}(t) = \psi_j(t)$.

(a) Show that this system has exactly $N$ linearly independent solutions.
(b) Find the $N$ normalized energy eigenstates $\psi_j^{(n)}(t)$ and their corresponding energy eigenvalues $E_n$. Verify that in the continuum limit (lattice spacing $\lambda \to 0$ with macroscopic length $L = N\lambda$ fixed), the energies reduce to the free particle relation $E = p^2/2m$.
(c) Derive an explicit expression for the elements of the propagator matrix $K_{jk}(t, t')$, defined by $\psi_j(t) = \sum_{k=1}^N K_{jk}(t, t') \psi_k(t')$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Discrete Schrödinger Equation**: The probability amplitudes for a particle on a discrete 1D lattice satisfy a set of coupled linear differential equations.
  $$ i \frac{d\psi_j(t)}{dt} = w [\psi_{j+1}(t) - 2\psi_j(t) + \psi_{j-1}(t)] + \frac{v_j}{\hbar} \psi_j(t) $$
- **Continuum Limit**: As the lattice spacing $\lambda \to 0$, the hopping parameter $w$ is matched to the physical mass $m$ to recover the continuous kinetic energy operator.
  $$ w \lambda^2 \to -\frac{\hbar}{2m} $$
- **Propagator Definition**: The propagator $K(t, t')$ evolves the state vector forward in time. In the energy eigenbasis, its matrix elements are given by:
  $$ K_{jk}(t, t') = \sum_n \braket{j}{n} \braket{n}{k} e^{-i E_n (t-t')/\hbar} $$

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — For part (a), recognize that the discrete Schrödinger equation represents a system of $N$ coupled linear, first-order ordinary differential equations. Alternatively, look for plane wave "normal mode" solutions compatible with the periodic boundary conditions.

> **Hint 2** — For part (b), try a spatial ansatz of the form $\psi_j \propto e^{i k j \lambda}$. Substitute this into the Schrödinger equation to find the dispersion relation for the allowed frequencies $\omega_n$, and use $E_n = \hbar \omega_n$.

> **Hint 3** — For the continuum limit, expand the discrete energy dispersion $E_n$ for small wave vectors ($k \lambda \ll 1$) and apply the continuum matching condition for the hopping parameter $w \to -\hbar / (2m\lambda^2)$.

> **Hint 4** — For part (c), express an arbitrary initial state $\psi_k(t')$ as a superposition of the $N$ energy eigenstates. Time-evolve each component, and re-express the result as a matrix acting on the initial amplitudes to isolate $K_{jk}(t, t')$.

## Solution

### Step 1: Linear Independence of Solutions
The equation governing the amplitudes is:
$$ i \frac{d\psi_j(t)}{dt} = w [\psi_{j+1}(t) - 2\psi_j(t) + \psi_{j-1}(t)] $$
for $j = 1, \dots, N$. This constitutes a system of $N$ coupled, linear, first-order differential equations with constant coefficients. By the fundamental theorem of linear differential equations, such a system admits exactly $N$ linearly independent solutions. We can construct these explicitly by seeking stationary states of the form:
$$ \psi_j(t) = a_j e^{-i \omega t} $$
Substituting this ansatz into the Schrödinger equation yields the eigenvalue problem for the amplitudes:
$$ \omega a_j = w(a_{j+1} - 2a_j + a_{j-1}) $$
We look for plane wave solutions $a_j \propto e^{i \theta j}$. The periodic boundary condition $a_{j+N} = a_j$ requires:
$$ e^{i \theta (j+N)} = e^{i \theta j} \implies e^{i \theta N} = 1 $$
This limits the allowed phase shifts to $\theta_n = \frac{2\pi n}{N}$ for $n = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$. Since there are exactly $N$ distinct roots of unity, we obtain $N$ linearly independent normal mode solutions.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Normalized Energy Eigenstates and Energies
The spatial part of the $n$-th eigenstate corresponding to a fixed frequency $\omega_n$ is:
$$ \psi_j^{(n)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} e^{i \frac{2\pi n}{N} j} $$
The prefactor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$ ensures the state is normalized over the lattice: $\sum_{j=1}^N |\psi_j^{(n)}|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{1}{N} = 1$.
To find the possible energies $E_n = \hbar \omega_n$, we substitute $a_j = \psi_j^{(n)}$ back into the eigenvalue equation:
$$ \omega_n = w \left( e^{i \frac{2\pi n}{N}} - 2 + e^{-i \frac{2\pi n}{N}} \right) = w \left( 2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N}\right) - 2 \right) $$
Using the half-angle trigonometric identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$, we get:
$$ E_n = -4 \hbar w \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi n}{N}\right) $$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: The Continuum Limit
Let the lattice spacing be $\lambda$. The length of the system is $L = N\lambda$, which is held fixed as $\lambda \to 0$ and $N \to \infty$. 
We define the continuous wavevector $k = \frac{2\pi n}{L} = \frac{2\pi n}{N\lambda}$. 
The argument of the sine function can then be written as $\frac{\pi n}{N} = \frac{k \lambda}{2}$.
In the continuum limit $\lambda \to 0$, we have $k\lambda \ll 1$, allowing us to expand the sine function $\sin\left(\frac{k\lambda}{2}\right) \approx \frac{k\lambda}{2}$.
The energy spectrum in this limit becomes:
$$ E_n \approx -4\hbar w \left( \frac{k\lambda}{2} \right)^2 = -\hbar w k^2 \lambda^2 $$
As established in the text (Eq. 3-20), the hopping parameter must scale with $\lambda$ as $\lambda^2 w \to -\frac{\hbar}{2m}$ in order to match macroscopic physics. Substituting this identification, we obtain:
$$ E_n \to -\hbar \left( -\frac{\hbar}{2m} \right) k^2 = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m} $$
where $p = \hbar k$. This perfectly reproduces the continuum free particle energies.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: The Propagator Matrix
The most general solution for the wave function is a linear combination of all energy eigenstates:
$$ \psi_j(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n \psi_j^{(n)} e^{-i E_n t / \hbar} $$
Given an initial state $\psi_k(t')$ at time $t'$, we can determine the coefficients $c_n$:
$$ \psi_k(t') = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n \psi_k^{(n)} e^{-i E_n t' / \hbar} $$
Multiplying by $\left(\psi_k^{(m)}\right)^*$ and summing over all sites $k$, we use the orthonormality relation $\sum_{k=1}^N \left(\psi_k^{(m)}\right)^* \psi_k^{(n)} = \delta_{mn}$ to isolate $c_m$:
$$ c_n = e^{i E_n t' / \hbar} \sum_{k=1}^N \left(\psi_k^{(n)}\right)^* \psi_k(t') $$
Substituting this back into the expression for $\psi_j(t)$ gives:
$$ \psi_j(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \left( e^{i E_n t' / \hbar} \sum_{k=1}^N \left(\psi_k^{(n)}\right)^* \psi_k(t') \right) \psi_j^{(n)} e^{-i E_n t / \hbar} $$
Rearranging the sums allows us to extract the propagator $K_{jk}(t, t')$:
$$ \psi_j(t) = \sum_{k=1}^N \left[ \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \psi_j^{(n)} \left(\psi_k^{(n)}\right)^* e^{-i E_n (t-t') / \hbar} \right] \psi_k(t') $$
Thus, the propagator is:
$$ K_{jk}(t, t') = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \psi_j^{(n)} \left(\psi_k^{(n)}\right)^* e^{-i E_n (t-t') / \hbar} $$
Substituting our explicit forms for $\psi_j^{(n)}$ and $E_n$:
$$ K_{jk}(t, t') = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{i \frac{2\pi n}{N} (j-k)} e^{4 i w (t-t') \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi n}{N}\right)} $$
Equivalently, writing the exponent entirely in terms of cosines:
$$ K_{jk}(t, t') = \frac{e^{2i w (t-t')}}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \exp\left[ i \frac{2\pi n}{N} (j-k) - 2i w (t-t') \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N}\right) \right] $$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{ K_{jk}(t, t') = \frac{e^{2i w (t-t')}}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \exp\left[ i \frac{2\pi n}{N} (j-k) - 2i w (t-t') \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N}\right) \right] }
$$

## Discussion

The discrete Schrödinger equation on a lattice reveals a beautiful correspondence between the tight-binding model in solid state physics and the continuum free particle. The tight-binding energy band is given by $E(k) = -2\hbar w \cos(k\lambda) - 2\hbar w$ (after properly shifting the zero of energy). Near the bottom of the band ($k=0$), the dispersion relation is approximately parabolic, perfectly mimicking a non-relativistic free particle with an effective mass $m^* = \hbar / (2 w \lambda^2)$. 
The explicit calculation of the discrete propagator yields a finite sum that converges exactly to the free particle Gaussian propagator as $N \to \infty$ and $\lambda \to 0$. In the $N \to \infty$ limit, this sum transforms into an integral representation of the Bessel function $J_{j-k}(2w(t-t'))$.
