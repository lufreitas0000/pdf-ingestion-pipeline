# Problem 1.9

**Source**: Chapter 1, Problem 9

## Problem Statement

9. Photons polarized at 30° to the x axis are sent through a y-polaroid. An attempt is made to determine how frequently the photons that pass through the polaroid, pass through "as right circularly polarized photons," and how frequently they pass through "as left circularly polarized photons"; this attempt is made as follows:
First, a prism that passes only right circular polarized light is placed between the source of the 30° polarized photons and the y-polaroid, and it is determined how frequently the 30° photons pass through the y-polaroid. Then this experiment is repeated with a prism that passes only left circular polarized light instead of the one that passes only right. Show by explicit calculation that the sum of the probabilities for passing through the y-polaroid measured in these two experiments is different from the probability that one would measure if there were no prism in the path of the photon and only the y-polaroid.
Relate this experiment to the two-slit diffraction experiment.

---

## Restatement

Photons initially prepared in a linear polarization state at an angle of $30^\circ$ relative to the $x$-axis (denoted $\ket{30^\circ}$) are sent through a linear polarizer oriented along the $y$-axis (which passes only $\ket{y}$). We wish to compare two scenarios:
1. The photons pass directly from the source to the $y$-polarizer.
2. An intermediate measurement is performed to determine if the photons are right-circularly polarized ($\ket{R}$) or left-circularly polarized ($\ket{L}$). This is done by placing a prism that passes only $\ket{R}$ (Experiment 2a) or only $\ket{L}$ (Experiment 2b) between the source and the $y$-polarizer. 

We must calculate the total probability of passing the $y$-polarizer for both scenarios and show they are different. Finally, we relate this physical situation to the two-slit diffraction experiment.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Polarization states**: Linear basis states are $\ket{x}$ and $\ket{y}$. Circular basis states are $\ket{R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{x} + i\ket{y})$ and $\ket{L} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{x} - i\ket{y})$.
- **Arbitrary linear polarization**: A state polarized at angle $\theta$ to the $x$-axis is given by $\ket{\theta} = \cos\theta\ket{x} + \sin\theta\ket{y}$.
- **Transition probability**: The probability for a system in initial state $\ket{\psi}$ to be found in state $\ket{\phi}$ after measurement is $|\braket{\phi}{\psi}|^2$.
- **Quantum interference**: Amplitudes of indistinguishable paths add coherently, whereas probabilities of distinguishable paths add incoherently.

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Write out the initial state $\ket{30^\circ}$ explicitly in the $\ket{x}, \ket{y}$ basis.

> **Hint 2** — For the experiments with prisms, calculate the probability of the photon first passing the prism (e.g., $|\braket{R}{30^\circ}|^2$) and then the subsequent probability of passing the $y$-polaroid (e.g., $|\braket{y}{R}|^2$). Multiply these probabilities for a given path.

> **Hint 3** — Compare the coherent sum (amplitude squared) when no intermediate measurement is made to the incoherent sum (sum of probabilities squared) when the prism is introduced.

## Solution

### Step 1: Define the Initial and Final States
The initial photon state is linearly polarized at $30^\circ$ to the $x$-axis. We can write this in the standard basis:
$$
\ket{30^\circ} = \cos(30^\circ)\ket{x} + \sin(30^\circ)\ket{y} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ket{x} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{y}
$$
The final target state is passing the $y$-polaroid, which corresponds to the state $\ket{y}$. 
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Calculate the Probability with No Intermediate Prism
When no intermediate prism is present, we calculate the transition probability directly from the initial state to the final state.
The transition amplitude is:
$$
\braket{y}{30^\circ} = \bra{y} \left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ket{x} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{y} \right) = \frac{1}{2}
$$
The corresponding probability is:
$$
P_{\text{direct}} = |\braket{y}{30^\circ}|^2 = \left| \frac{1}{2} \right|^2 = \frac{1}{4}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Calculate the Probability for the Right-Circular (R) Prism
When the right-circular prism is inserted, the photon must first pass the prism (transitioning to $\ket{R}$) and then pass the $y$-polaroid.
The $\ket{R}$ state is defined as:
$$
\ket{R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{x} + i\ket{y})
$$
The amplitude to pass the $\ket{R}$ prism is:
$$
\braket{R}{30^\circ} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\bra{x} - i\bra{y}) \left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ket{x} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{y} \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{i}{2} \right)
$$
The probability to pass the $\ket{R}$ prism is:
$$
P(R|30^\circ) = |\braket{R}{30^\circ}|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{2}
$$
After passing the prism, the state is $\ket{R}$. The amplitude for this state to then pass the $y$-polaroid is:
$$
\braket{y}{R} = \bra{y} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{x} + i\ket{y}) \right) = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}
$$
The probability to pass the $y$-polaroid given state $\ket{R}$ is:
$$
P(y|R) = |\braket{y}{R}|^2 = \frac{1}{2}
$$
The total probability for a photon to pass through the $\ket{R}$ prism and then the $y$-polaroid is the product of these probabilities:
$$
P_{R} = P(y|R) P(R|30^\circ) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Calculate the Probability for the Left-Circular (L) Prism
Similarly, for the left-circular prism, the $\ket{L}$ state is:
$$
\ket{L} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{x} - i\ket{y})
$$
The amplitude to pass the $\ket{L}$ prism is:
$$
\braket{L}{30^\circ} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\bra{x} + i\bra{y}) \left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ket{x} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{y} \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{i}{2} \right)
$$
The probability to pass the $\ket{L}$ prism is:
$$
P(L|30^\circ) = |\braket{L}{30^\circ}|^2 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{1}{2}
$$
After passing the prism, the amplitude to pass the $y$-polaroid is:
$$
\braket{y}{L} = \bra{y} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{x} - i\ket{y}) \right) = -\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}
$$
The probability to pass the $y$-polaroid given state $\ket{L}$ is:
$$
P(y|L) = |\braket{y}{L}|^2 = \frac{1}{2}
$$
The total probability for a photon to pass through the $\ket{L}$ prism and then the $y$-polaroid is:
$$
P_{L} = P(y|L) P(L|30^\circ) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 5: Compare the Results
The sum of the probabilities from the two separate prism experiments is:
$$
P_{\text{sum}} = P_R + P_L = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}
$$
However, as calculated in Step 2, the probability when there is no prism is:
$$
P_{\text{direct}} = \frac{1}{4}
$$
Comparing the two results, we see that $P_{\text{sum}} \neq P_{\text{direct}}$. The intermediate measurement fundamentally alters the statistics of the outcome.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{ P_{\text{sum}} = \frac{1}{2} \neq \frac{1}{4} = P_{\text{direct}} }
$$

## Discussion

This thought experiment elegantly demonstrates the effect of measurement in quantum mechanics, serving as a miniature version of the measurement problem. When the photon passes directly from the source to the $y$-polaroid, we do not know whether it "traveled" as a right-circular or left-circular photon. Since the "which-path" information is unknown, we must sum the probability amplitudes *coherently* before squaring: $P \propto |\alpha_R + \alpha_L|^2$. 

Inserting the R or L prism constitutes a measurement—it extracts "which-path" information, forcing the system into a definite circular polarization state. This collapses the superposition, destroying the interference between the R and L paths. The rules for combining probabilities change to an *incoherent* sum of individual probabilities: $P = |\alpha_R|^2 + |\alpha_L|^2$. 

This is perfectly analogous to the two-slit diffraction experiment. In the two-slit setup, if we do not observe which slit the particle goes through, the paths interfere, producing a diffraction pattern (like the coherent sum yielding 1/4). If we place detectors at the slits to determine which slit the particle went through, the interference pattern is destroyed, and the resulting distribution is just the classical sum of the two independent single-slit patterns (like the incoherent sum yielding 1/2). In both cases, gaining "which-path" information destroys quantum interference.

## Related Resources

- **Typical method used**: Direct calculation of transition probabilities in bra-ket notation, decomposing intermediate paths using projection operators.
- **Alternative approaches**: The density matrix formalism could be used. In the direct case, a pure state $\ket{30^\circ}\bra{30^\circ}$ undergoes projection onto $\ket{y}$. In the prism case, the measurement acts as a decoherence mechanism, transforming the pure state into a mixed state $\frac{1}{2}\ket{R}\bra{R} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{L}\bra{L}$, before projecting onto $\ket{y}$.
