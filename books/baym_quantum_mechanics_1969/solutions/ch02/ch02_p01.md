# Problem 2.1

**Source**: Chapter 2, Problem 1

## Problem Statement

Suppose that the beam of K mesons passed through the two components of the apparatus in Figure 2-1 very rapidly compared to the lifetime, $\tau_1$. How does the probability that a $K$ emerges vary with the proper time $T$ that the K spends between the two components?

---

## Restatement

Consider a beam of neutral K mesons ($K^0$, denoted simply as $K$). The state $K$ is a superposition of the vacuum eigenstates $K_1$ and $K_2$:
$$|K\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|K_1\rangle + |K_2\rangle)$$
where $K_1$ and $K_2$ have masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, and lifetimes $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$, respectively.
The beam passes through an apparatus that acts as a "K-polaroid", absorbing anti-K mesons ($\bar{K}$) and transmitting only $K$. If the beam travels between two such components over a proper time $T$ that is very small compared to the shorter lifetime $\tau_1$ (so that decays can be ignored), what is the probability that the particle emerges as a $K$?

---

## Prerequisites

- **Superposition in Two-State Systems**: A generic state can be expanded in terms of the energy eigenstates of the system.
- **Time Evolution of Stationary States**: An energy eigenstate $|E\rangle$ evolves in time as $|E(t)\rangle = e^{-i E t / \hbar} |E\rangle$. For proper time in the rest frame, the energy is the mass $m$ (with $c=1, \hbar=1$).
- **Probability Amplitude**: The probability of finding a system in state $|\phi\rangle$ given it is in state $|\psi\rangle$ is $P = |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2$.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Express the initial state of the K meson in the basis of the mass eigenstates $K_1$ and $K_2$.

> **Hint 2** — Apply the time evolution operator to each mass eigenstate separately. Since $T \ll \tau_1$, you can neglect the exponential decay factors $e^{-T/2\tau_1}$ and $e^{-T/2\tau_2}$.

> **Hint 3** — Take the inner product of the time-evolved state with the original $K$ state to find the probability amplitude, and square its magnitude to find the probability. Use the identity $1 + \cos(x) = 2\cos^2(x/2)$ to simplify the result.

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Initial State Preparation
The K meson ($K^0$) is prepared at proper time $T=0$. The state can be expanded in the basis of the vacuum mass eigenstates $|K_1\rangle$ and $|K_2\rangle$:
$$
|K(0)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|K_1\rangle + |K_2\rangle)
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Time Evolution
The system evolves for a proper time $T$ in the vacuum between the two components. The time evolution of the mass eigenstates in their rest frame is given by:
$$
|K_1(T)\rangle = e^{-i m_1 T} e^{-T/2\tau_1} |K_1\rangle
$$
$$
|K_2(T)\rangle = e^{-i m_2 T} e^{-T/2\tau_2} |K_2\rangle
$$
Because the time $T$ is very rapid compared to the lifetime $\tau_1$ ($T \ll \tau_1 \ll \tau_2$), we can approximate $e^{-T/2\tau_1} \approx 1$ and $e^{-T/2\tau_2} \approx 1$. The state at time $T$ is then:
$$
|\Psi(T)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( e^{-i m_1 T} |K_1\rangle + e^{-i m_2 T} |K_2\rangle \right)
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Probability Amplitude
The second component of the apparatus transmits only the $K$ state. The probability amplitude for the particle to emerge as $K$ is the projection of $|\Psi(T)\rangle$ onto $|K\rangle$:
$$
\langle K | \Psi(T)\rangle = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \langle K_1| + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \langle K_2| \right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( e^{-i m_1 T} |K_1\rangle + e^{-i m_2 T} |K_2\rangle \right)
$$
Since $|K_1\rangle$ and $|K_2\rangle$ are orthonormal ($\langle K_i | K_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$):
$$
\langle K | \Psi(T)\rangle = \frac{1}{2} \left( e^{-i m_1 T} + e^{-i m_2 T} \right)
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Emergence Probability
The probability of the particle emerging as a $K$ is the absolute square of the probability amplitude:
$$
P(K) = |\langle K | \Psi(T)\rangle|^2 = \frac{1}{4} |e^{-i m_1 T} + e^{-i m_2 T}|^2
$$
Expanding the magnitude squared:
$$
P(K) = \frac{1}{4} \left( e^{-i m_1 T} + e^{-i m_2 T} \right) \left( e^{i m_1 T} + e^{i m_2 T} \right)
$$
$$
P(K) = \frac{1}{4} \left( 1 + 1 + e^{i(m_1 - m_2)T} + e^{-i(m_1 - m_2)T} \right)
$$
Using Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos(\theta)$:
$$
P(K) = \frac{1}{4} \left( 2 + 2\cos((m_1 - m_2)T) \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( 1 + \cos((m_1 - m_2)T) \right)
$$
Using the half-angle identity $1 + \cos(\theta) = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$:
$$
P(K) = \cos^2\left( \frac{(m_1 - m_2)T}{2} \right)
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{ P(K) = \cos^2\left( \frac{(m_1 - m_2)T}{2} \right) }
$$

---

## Discussion

The result demonstrates quantum interference between the two mass eigenstates $|K_1\rangle$ and $|K_2\rangle$. Even though the initial state is entirely $K$, the slight difference in mass $\Delta m = m_1 - m_2$ causes the two components to accumulate phase at different rates. This relative phase shift leads to a non-zero probability of finding the system in the $\bar{K}$ state (which would be absorbed). The probability oscillates sinusoidally with proper time $T$, representing the phenomenon of kaon oscillations. If $\Delta m$ were zero, the state would remain pure $K$ indefinitely (ignoring decay).

## Notes

The problem assumes an idealized setup where the lifetime is much longer than the time of flight between the components. In realistic experiments, the decay terms must be included, which leads to exponential damping of both the average probability and the interference oscillations.
