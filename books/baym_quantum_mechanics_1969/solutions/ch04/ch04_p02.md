# Problem 4.2

**Source**: Chapter 4, Problem 2

## Problem Statement

2. Consider the potential in Problem 1. Assume that a wave packet with $E_0 = p_0^2/2m < V$ is incident on the barrier from the left. Calculate in terms of $E_0$ and $V$ the difference in time between the arrival of the incident packet at the step and the departure of the reflected packet from the step. Relate this time delay to a "distance of travel" within the step.

---

## Restatement

Consider a one-dimensional potential step $V(x) = V \Theta(x)$, where $\Theta(x)$ is the Heaviside step function. A wave packet with central energy $E_0 = p_0^2/2m < V$ is incident on the step from the left ($x < 0$). 
Calculate the time delay $\Delta t$ between the arrival of the incident wave packet at the step ($x = 0$) and the departure of the reflected wave packet from the step. Express $\Delta t$ in terms of $E_0$ and $V$. Finally, relate this time delay to an effective "distance of travel" within the classically forbidden region $x > 0$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Stationary phase approximation**: The center of a wave packet defined by $\int \dd p \, f(p) e^{i\phi(p)}$ follows the trajectory where the phase is stationary: $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial p} = 0$.
- **Reflection from a step ($E < V$)**: The reflection coefficient is a pure phase $B/A = -e^{2i\delta(E)}$, where $\cot \delta = \frac{\hbar\kappa}{p} = \sqrt{\frac{V-E}{E}}$.
- **Penetration depth**: In the classically forbidden region ($E < V$), the wave function decays exponentially as $e^{-\kappa x}$ with decay constant $\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V-E)}}{\hbar}$.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Write down the incident and reflected wave packets as superpositions of momentum eigenstates. Identify the total phase of each integrand.
> **Hint 2** — Apply the stationary phase condition to both wave packets evaluated at the central momentum $p_0$. This will yield the classical trajectories of the wave packet centers.
> **Hint 3** — Use the boundary conditions at $x=0$ to express the phase shift $\delta(E)$ of the reflected amplitude analytically in terms of $E$ and $V$.
> **Hint 4** — The time delay $\Delta t$ is proportional to the energy derivative of the phase shift, $d\delta/dE$. Calculate this derivative.
> **Hint 5** — Multiply the time delay by the incident velocity $v_0 = p_0/m$ to obtain an effective distance. Compare this distance to the penetration depth $1/\kappa$ of the evanescent wave inside the step.

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Incident and Reflected Wave Packets

The incident wave packet can be written as a superposition of plane waves traveling to the right:
$$
\psi_{\text{inc}}(x, t) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\dd p}{2\pi\hbar} f(p) e^{i(px - Et)/\hbar}
$$
where $E = p^2/2m$ and $f(p)$ is a real amplitude sharply peaked around the central momentum $p_0 = \sqrt{2mE_0}$. The total phase of the integrand is $\phi_{\text{inc}}(p) = (px - Et)/\hbar$.

For $E < V$, the energy eigenstates have the form:
$$
\psi_E(x) = \begin{cases} A e^{ipx/\hbar} + B e^{-ipx/\hbar} & x < 0 \\ C e^{-\kappa x} & x > 0 \end{cases}
$$
where $\hbar \kappa = \sqrt{2m(V-E)}$. Since all particles must be reflected, $|B| = |A|$. We define the reflection coefficient in terms of a phase shift $\delta(E)$:
$$
\frac{B}{A} = -e^{2i\delta(E)} = e^{i(2\delta(E) + \pi)}
$$
Thus, the reflected wave packet is given by:
$$
\psi_{\text{refl}}(x, t) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\dd p}{2\pi\hbar} f(p) e^{i\phi_{\text{refl}}(p)}
$$
where the total phase includes the reflection phase shift:
$$
\phi_{\text{refl}}(p) = \frac{-px - Et}{\hbar} + 2\delta(E) + \pi
$$

### Step 2: Extracting the Time Delay via Stationary Phase

The center of each wave packet follows the trajectory defined by the stationary phase condition $\partial \phi / \partial p = 0$ evaluated at the central momentum $p_0$.

For the incident packet:
$$
\frac{\partial \phi_{\text{inc}}}{\partial p} = \frac{x}{\hbar} - \frac{t}{\hbar} \frac{dE}{dp} = 0
$$
Since $dE/dp = p/m = v$, the incident packet follows $x = v_0 t$. It arrives at the step $x=0$ at the arrival time $t_{\text{arr}} = 0$.

For the reflected packet:
$$
\frac{\partial \phi_{\text{refl}}}{\partial p} = -\frac{x}{\hbar} - \frac{t}{\hbar} \frac{dE}{dp} + 2 \frac{d\delta}{dE} \frac{dE}{dp} = 0
$$
Using $dE/dp = v$, we find the trajectory of the reflected packet:
$$
-x - v t + 2\hbar v \frac{d\delta}{dE} = 0 \implies x = -v \left( t - 2\hbar \frac{d\delta}{dE} \right)
$$
The center of the reflected wave packet is at $x=0$ at the departure time $t_{\text{dep}} = 2\hbar \frac{d\delta}{dE}$.
Therefore, the time delay between arrival and departure is:
$$
\Delta t = t_{\text{dep}} - t_{\text{arr}} = 2\hbar \frac{d\delta}{dE} \bigg|_{E=E_0}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Finding the Phase Shift $\delta(E)$

Matching the wave function and its derivative at $x=0$ gives the ratio:
$$
\frac{B}{A} = \frac{p - i\hbar\kappa}{p + i\hbar\kappa}
$$
We equate this to our parameterization $-e^{2i\delta}$:
$$
e^{2i\delta} = \frac{-p + i\hbar\kappa}{p + i\hbar\kappa}
$$
Let the denominator be $p + i\hbar\kappa = R e^{i\theta}$, where $\tan\theta = \hbar\kappa/p$. Then the numerator is $-p + i\hbar\kappa = -R e^{-i\theta}$.
$$
e^{2i\delta} = \frac{-R e^{-i\theta}}{R e^{i\theta}} = -e^{-2i\theta} = e^{i(\pi - 2\theta)}
$$
Comparing phases, we have $2\delta = \pi - 2\theta$, which means $\delta = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$. Therefore:
$$
\tan \delta = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \cot \theta = \frac{p}{\hbar\kappa}
$$
Substituting $p = \sqrt{2mE}$ and $\hbar\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V-E)}$, we find:
$$
\tan \delta = \sqrt{\frac{E}{V-E}} \implies \delta(E) = \arctan\left( \sqrt{\frac{E}{V-E}} \right)
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Computing the Time Delay $\Delta t$

Now we compute the derivative $d\delta/dE$:
$$
\frac{d\delta}{dE} = \frac{d}{dE} \arctan\left( \sqrt{\frac{E}{V-E}} \right) = \frac{1}{1 + \left(\sqrt{\frac{E}{V-E}}\right)^2} \cdot \frac{d}{dE} \left( \sqrt{\frac{E}{V-E}} \right)
$$
The prefactor simplifies to:
$$
\frac{1}{1 + \frac{E}{V-E}} = \frac{V-E}{V}
$$
The derivative of the argument is:
$$
\frac{d}{dE} \left( \frac{E}{V-E} \right)^{1/2} = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\frac{V-E}{E}} \cdot \frac{(V-E) - E(-1)}{(V-E)^2} = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\frac{V-E}{E}} \frac{V}{(V-E)^2}
$$
Multiplying these together:
$$
\frac{d\delta}{dE} = \left( \frac{V-E}{V} \right) \left( \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\frac{V-E}{E}} \frac{V}{(V-E)^2} \right) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{E(V-E)}}
$$
Evaluating this at $E = E_0$, the time delay is:
$$
\Delta t = 2\hbar \left( \frac{1}{2\sqrt{E_0(V-E_0)}} \right) = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{E_0(V-E_0)}}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 5: Relating to a "Distance of Travel"

Let us interpret this time delay as an effective distance traveled by the classical particle at its incident speed $v_0 = p_0/m = \sqrt{2E_0/m}$.
The corresponding distance is:
$$
\text{Distance} = v_0 \Delta t = \sqrt{\frac{2E_0}{m}} \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{E_0(V-E_0)}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\hbar}{\sqrt{m(V-E_0)}}
$$
Recall that the characteristic penetration depth of the evanescent wave into the step is $d = 1/\kappa$, where $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V-E_0)}/\hbar$.
We can rewrite the distance in terms of $\kappa$:
$$
\text{Distance} = \frac{2\hbar}{\sqrt{2m(V-E_0)}} = \frac{2}{\kappa} = 2d
$$
Thus, the time delay is exactly the time it would take a classical particle to travel a distance $1/\kappa$ into the barrier and $1/\kappa$ back out at its incident velocity.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{\Delta t = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{E_0(V-E_0)}}, \quad \text{Distance} = v_0 \Delta t = \frac{2}{\kappa}}
$$

## Discussion

The time delay calculation beautifully highlights a phenomenon sometimes called the Wigner time delay. Although the particle does not have enough energy to classically propagate in the region $x > 0$, the reflection is not strictly instantaneous. Quantum mechanically, the wave packet "lingers" at the barrier boundary. 
The effective distance calculation shows that this lingering time perfectly maps to a semi-classical trajectory where the particle penetrates into the forbidden region up to its characteristic decay depth $1/\kappa$, turns around, and travels back out, all while moving at its incident classical velocity $v_0$. 
This time delay diverges both as $E_0 \to 0$ (because the particle is extremely slow) and as $E_0 \to V$ (because the barrier becomes effectively transparent, causing $\kappa \to 0$ and the penetration depth to diverge).
