# Problem 4.1

**Source**: Chapter 4, Problem 1

## Problem Statement

1. Why are there no solutions for $E < 0$ for the barrier problem,
$$
V(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < 0 \\
V > 0, & x > 0?
\end{cases}
$$

---

## Restatement

Consider a particle of mass $m$ subject to a one-dimensional potential step given by
$$
V(x) = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{for } x < 0, \\
V_0 & \text{for } x > 0,
\end{cases}
$$
where $V_0 > 0$. Show that the time-independent Schrödinger equation admits no physically acceptable non-trivial solutions for energy $E < 0$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Time-independent Schrödinger Equation**: The fundamental equation for stationary states, $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi$.
- **Boundary Conditions at Infinity**: Normalizable or physically acceptable wavefunctions must not diverge as $x \to \pm \infty$.
- **Continuity Conditions**: The wavefunction $\psi(x)$ and its first derivative $\psi'(x)$ must be continuous at boundaries where the potential has a finite jump.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Write down the general solutions to the Schrödinger equation for the regions $x < 0$ and $x > 0$ under the assumption that $E < 0$.

> **Hint 2** — Apply the boundary conditions as $x \to \pm\infty$ to eliminate the terms in the general solution that diverge.

> **Hint 3** — Impose the continuity of the wavefunction and its derivative at $x = 0$ and show that the only possible solution is the trivial one ($\psi(x) = 0$).

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Solving the Schrödinger equation in each region

For a stationary state with energy $E < 0$, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi.
$$

**Region I ($x < 0$):**
Here $V(x) = 0$. The equation becomes
$$
\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2} = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\psi = \kappa^2 \psi,
$$
where we have defined $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$. Since $E < 0$, $\kappa$ is a strictly positive real number.
The general solution in this region is
$$
\psi_I(x) = A e^{\kappa x} + B e^{-\kappa x}.
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

**Region II ($x > 0$):**
Here $V(x) = V_0 > 0$. The equation becomes
$$
\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2} = \frac{2m(V_0 - E)}{\hbar^2}\psi = \kappa'^2 \psi,
$$
where $\kappa' = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$. Since $V_0 > 0$ and $E < 0$, we have $V_0 - E > 0$, so $\kappa'$ is also a strictly positive real number.
The general solution in this region is
$$
\psi_{II}(x) = C e^{\kappa' x} + D e^{-\kappa' x}.
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Applying boundary conditions at infinity

A physically acceptable wavefunction must remain bounded as $x \to \pm \infty$.

In Region I ($x < 0$), the term $e^{-\kappa x}$ diverges as $x \to -\infty$ since $\kappa > 0$. Therefore, we must set $B = 0$. This leaves:
$$
\psi_I(x) = A e^{\kappa x}.
$$

In Region II ($x > 0$), the term $e^{\kappa' x}$ diverges as $x \to +\infty$ since $\kappa' > 0$. Therefore, we must set $C = 0$. This leaves:
$$
\psi_{II}(x) = D e^{-\kappa' x}.
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Imposing continuity at $x=0$

The potential has a finite jump at $x = 0$, so both the wavefunction and its first derivative must be continuous at this point.

Continuity of the wavefunction at $x = 0$ gives:
$$
\psi_I(0) = \psi_{II}(0) \implies A = D.
$$

Continuity of the first derivative at $x = 0$ gives:
$$
\psi_I'(0) = \psi_{II}'(0) \implies \kappa A = -\kappa' D.
$$

Substituting $D = A$ into the derivative matching equation yields:
$$
\kappa A = -\kappa' A \implies (\kappa + \kappa') A = 0.
$$

Since both $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ are strictly positive, their sum $\kappa + \kappa'$ is strictly positive. Therefore, the only way this equation can be satisfied is if:
$$
A = 0.
$$

Consequently, $D = A = 0$. Thus, $\psi(x) = 0$ everywhere, which is the trivial solution. There are no physically meaningful eigenstates for $E < 0$.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{\text{There are no non-trivial solutions because matching boundary conditions requires } A(\kappa + \kappa') = 0 \text{, which implies } \psi(x) = 0.}
$$

---

## Discussion

This result illustrates a general property of quantum mechanical systems: the energy $E$ of any physical state must be strictly greater than the global minimum of the potential energy, $E > \min(V(x))$.

This can be seen readily from the expectation value of the Hamiltonian. For any normalized state $\ket{\psi}$:
$$
\langle E \rangle = \bra{\psi}\hat{H}\ket{\psi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^*(x) \left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2} + V(x) \right) \psi(x) \, \dd x.
$$
Integrating the kinetic energy term by parts gives:
$$
\langle E \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left( \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \left| \frac{\dd\psi}{\dd x} \right|^2 + V(x) |\psi(x)|^2 \right) \, \dd x.
$$
Since the kinetic energy density is non-negative and $V(x) \ge 0$ everywhere, the integral must be positive. Furthermore, unless $\psi(x)$ is a constant (which cannot be normalized), the kinetic energy is strictly greater than zero. Therefore, $E > 0$, confirming that no energy eigenstates exist for $E < 0$.
