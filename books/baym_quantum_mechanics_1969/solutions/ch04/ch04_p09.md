# Problem 4.9

**Source**: Chapter 4, Problem 9

## Problem Statement

9. An $\alpha$ particle emitter consisting of $N$ atoms, obeying an exponential decay law ($\sim e^{-\gamma t}$), is put into place at $t = 0$. Assume for simplicity that the particles are all emitted in the positive $x$ direction and with velocity $v$. Neglect the size of the emitter. Calculate the density $n(x, t)$ of emitted $\alpha$ particles. Sketch the result as a function of $x$ at several successive times, including $t > \gamma^{-1} \log N$. Relate this result to the wave functions of the emitted $\alpha$ particles.

---

## Restatement

Consider a point-like $\alpha$ particle emitter located at $x = 0$ containing $N$ radioactive atoms at time $t = 0$. The atoms undergo exponential decay, meaning the expected number of un-decayed atoms at time $t$ is $N(t) = N e^{-\gamma t}$. Upon decay, each atom emits an $\alpha$ particle that travels in the positive $x$ direction with a constant velocity $v$. Calculate the continuous linear number density $n(x, t)$ of the emitted $\alpha$ particles for $t > 0$. Describe the shape of $n(x, t)$ at successive times, specifically examining the late-time regime where $t > \gamma^{-1} \log N$. Finally, explain how this macroscopic particle density connects to the spatial structure of the single-particle quantum mechanical wave function of an emitted $\alpha$ particle.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Exponential Decay**: The rate at which particles are emitted from a decaying source is proportional to the number of remaining source atoms, $R(t) = -\frac{\dd N}{\dd t} = N\gamma e^{-\gamma t}$.
- **Kinematics and Continuity**: For particles moving at constant velocity $v$, a particle at position $x$ at time $t$ was emitted at retarded time $t' = t - x/v$. The linear density $n(x,t)$ relates to the emission rate by $n(x,t) = \frac{1}{v}R(t - x/v)$.
- **Decay Resonance Wavefunction**: The wavefunction of a particle leaking out of a quasibound state (a resonance of width $\Gamma = \hbar \gamma$) exhibits an "exponential horn" spatial envelope $\psi(x,t) \propto \exp\left[-\frac{\Gamma}{2\hbar}\left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)\right] \exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}(px - Et)\right]$.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Determine the emission rate $R(t)$ of $\alpha$ particles leaving the emitter at $x=0$ as a function of time.

> **Hint 2** — Trace back a particle found at $(x, t)$ to its time of emission $t'$. Use the conservation of particle number $\dd N = n(x, t) \, \dd x = R(t') \, \dd t'$ to find $n(x, t)$. 

> **Hint 3** — Remember that emission starts at $t=0$, so there is a sharp leading edge to the particle distribution. What is the maximum $x$ a particle can reach by time $t$?

> **Hint 4** — When relating this to quantum mechanics, assume the macroscopic density $n(x, t)$ is proportional to the probability density $|\psi(x, t)|^2$ of a single emitted particle. Deduce the shape of the amplitude envelope $|\psi(x,t)|$.

## Solution

### Step 1: Emission Rate
The number of un-decayed atoms in the emitter at time $t \ge 0$ is governed by the exponential decay law:
$$
N(t) = N e^{-\gamma t}
$$
The rate at which $\alpha$ particles are emitted is the negative derivative of $N(t)$:
$$
R(t) = -\frac{\dd N(t)}{\dd t} = N \gamma e^{-\gamma t}
$$
This rate represents the number of particles passing $x=0$ per unit time. 
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Kinematics and Density $n(x, t)$
Since the $\alpha$ particles are emitted in the positive $x$ direction with constant velocity $v$, a particle located at position $x$ at time $t$ must have been emitted at an earlier retarded time $t'$:
$$
t' = t - \frac{x}{v}
$$
Since the emitter is put into place and begins emitting at $t=0$, we must have $t' \ge 0$. This imposes the condition $x \le vt$. Furthermore, particles only travel in the positive $x$ direction, so $x \ge 0$. 

Consider the particles in a small spatial interval $\dd x$ at time $t$. The number of particles in this interval is $n(x, t) \, \dd x$. These same particles were emitted during a small time interval $\dd t'$ at the source. Differentiating the kinematic relation $x = v(t - t')$ at a fixed time $t$ gives $\dd x = v \, \dd t'$.
Equating the number of particles:
$$
n(x, t) \, \dd x = R(t') \, \dd t' \implies n(x, t) = \frac{R(t')}{v}
$$
Substituting $t' = t - x/v$ and the expression for $R(t')$, we find the density:
$$
n(x, t) = \frac{N \gamma}{v} e^{-\gamma \left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)} \quad \text{for} \quad 0 < x < vt
$$
Outside this region, $n(x, t) = 0$.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Evolution and Late-Time Behavior
At any given time $t > 0$, the density profile $n(x,t)$ is an exponentially increasing function of $x$ over the interval $[0, vt]$.
- The leading edge of the distribution is at $x = vt$, corresponding to the first particles emitted at $t=0$. The density at this front is constant over time: $n(vt, t) = \frac{N\gamma}{v}$.
- The trailing edge is at the emitter $x = 0$, where the density matches the continuously decreasing emission rate: $n(0, t) = \frac{N\gamma}{v}e^{-\gamma t}$.

As time $t$ progresses, the leading edge $x=vt$ moves to the right at speed $v$, while the density at $x=0$ drops exponentially. The entire profile looks like a "horn" that stretches out indefinitely.
For late times $t > \gamma^{-1} \log N$, the condition $\gamma t > \log N$ implies that $N e^{-\gamma t} < 1$. In this regime, the expected number of atoms remaining in the emitter is less than one. The fluid approximation of a continuous density breaks down physically (as fractional particles cannot be emitted), but mathematically, $n(0, t) < \gamma/v$, meaning the tail of the density near the emitter has become vanishingly small, and the bulk of the emitted probability is far away.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Relation to Quantum Wave Functions
In a quantum mechanical description, the macroscopic particle density $n(x, t)$ of an ensemble of non-interacting particles is proportional to the probability density $|\psi(x, t)|^2 = |\braket{x}{\psi(t)}|^2$ of the single-particle state $\ket{\psi(t)}$.
$$
|\psi(x, t)|^2 \propto n(x, t) \propto e^{-\gamma \left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)}
$$
Taking the square root to find the magnitude of the wave function:
$$
|\psi(x, t)| \propto e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2} \left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)}
$$
Including the standard oscillatory phase $e^{i(px - Et)/\hbar}$ for a free particle traveling with momentum $p = mv$ and energy $E = p^2/2m$, the full wave function of the emitted $\alpha$ particle takes the form:
$$
\psi(x, t) \sim \exp\left[ \frac{i}{\hbar}(px - Et) \right] \exp\left[ -\frac{\gamma}{2} \left( t - \frac{x}{v} \right) \right] \Theta(x) \Theta(vt - x)
$$
This structure precisely matches the wave function of a particle escaping from a quasibound state (a transmission resonance) derived via the Gamow theory of alpha decay. The decay rate $\gamma$ is related to the resonance width $\Gamma$ by $\gamma = \Gamma/\hbar$. The exponential decay in time of the parent state dictates the spatial "exponential horn" shape of the emitted wave packet.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{
n(x, t) = \begin{cases}
\frac{N \gamma}{v} e^{-\gamma \left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)} & \text{for } 0 < x < vt \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
}
$$

## Discussion

The exact equivalence between the classical ensemble density of emitted particles and the quantum mechanical probability density of a single decaying state highlights the consistency of the probabilistic interpretation of the wave function. The sharp leading wavefront at $x=vt$ is a consequence of the idealized sharp preparation of the system at $t=0$. In reality, the wave function would smooth out slightly at the front due to dispersion (the momentum is uncertain by $\Delta p \sim m \Gamma / 2p$), but for narrow resonances ($\Gamma \ll E$), the dispersion is negligible, and the wave packet moves bodily with velocity $v$ while maintaining its exponential shape.
