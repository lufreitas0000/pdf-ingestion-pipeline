# Problem 4.5

**Source**: Chapter 4, Problem 5

## Problem Statement

5. Consider a potential
$$
V_a(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < -a \\
V(x), & -a < x < a \\
0, & x > a
\end{cases}
$$
where $V(x)$ is some arbitrary function. Consider a wave incident from the left. Suppose that the reflection amplitude at $x = -a$ is $N(E)$ [Eq. (4-35)] (where $E$ is positive) and that the transmission amplitude, defined as the ratio of the transmitted wave at $x = a$ to the incident wave at $x = -a$, is $S(E)$. Now consider a wave incident from the right. Show that the transmission amplitude, now defined as the ratio of the transmitted wave at $-a$ to the incident wave at $a$, is also $S(E)$. Express the reflection amplitude at $a$ in terms of $N$ and $S$. (*Hint:* the Schrödinger equation in this case is a *real* equation.)

---

## Restatement

Consider a localized 1D potential $V(x)$ that is non-zero only in the region $-a < x < a$.
For a state with energy $E > 0$ incident from the left, let $N$ be the reflection amplitude measured at $x = -a$ and $S$ be the transmission amplitude measured as the ratio of the wave at $x = a$ to the incident wave at $x = -a$. Let $N'$ and $S'$ be the analogous reflection and transmission amplitudes for a wave incident from the right.

Show that the right-incident transmission amplitude is equal to the left-incident transmission amplitude ($S' = S$). Express the right-incident reflection amplitude $N'$ in terms of $N$ and $S$.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation**: For real potentials $V(x)$, if $\psi(x)$ is a steady-state solution with energy $E$, its complex conjugate $\psi^*(x)$ is also a solution with the same energy.
- **Probability Current Conservation**: The steady-state probability current $j(x) = \frac{\hbar}{2mi} \left( \psi^* \frac{\dd\psi}{\dd x} - \psi \frac{\dd\psi^*}{\dd x} \right)$ is spatially uniform, implying the conservation of probability flux ($|S|^2 + |N|^2 = 1$).
- **Linear Independence of Solutions**: The general solution to a second-order ODE at energy $E$ can be written as a linear combination of two linearly independent solutions (e.g., $\psi$ and $\psi^*$).

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — Write down the asymptotic forms of the wavefunctions for two distinct solutions: $\psi_L(x)$ (incident from the left) and $\psi_R(x)$ (incident from the right).

> **Hint 2** — Because the potential $V(x)$ is purely real, taking the complex conjugate of $\psi_L(x)$ generates another valid solution to the Schrödinger equation, $\psi_L^*(x)$.

> **Hint 3** — The right-incident solution $\psi_R(x)$ must be a linear combination of the two independent solutions $\psi_L(x)$ and $\psi_L^*(x)$. Write $\psi_R(x) = A\psi_L(x) + B\psi_L^*(x)$.

> **Hint 4** — Match the asymptotic pieces for $x > a$ to find the constants $A$ and $B$, then plug them into the $x < -a$ equations to find $S'$ and $N'$.

> **Hint 5** — Simplify the resulting expression for $S'$ using the conservation of probability current.

---

## Solution

### Step 1: Define the Asymptotic Solutions

Let $\psi_L(x)$ be the solution corresponding to a wave incident from the left. By the definition of the amplitudes $N$ and $S$:
- The incident wave for $x < -a$ has the form $e^{ik(x+a)}$, so its value at $x = -a$ is $1$.
- The reflected wave at $x = -a$ has amplitude $N$, taking the form $N e^{-ik(x+a)}$.
- The transmitted wave at $x = a$ has amplitude $S$ relative to the incident wave at $x = -a$, giving the form $S e^{ik(x-a)}$.

Thus, the asymptotic form of $\psi_L(x)$ is:
$$
\psi_L(x) = \begin{cases}
e^{ik(x+a)} + N e^{-ik(x+a)}, & x < -a \\
S e^{ik(x-a)}, & x > a
\end{cases}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

Let $\psi_R(x)$ be the solution for a wave incident from the right. Following the identical conventions for the right-incident amplitudes $N'$ and $S'$:
$$
\psi_R(x) = \begin{cases}
S' e^{-ik(x+a)}, & x < -a \\
e^{-ik(x-a)} + N' e^{ik(x-a)}, & x > a
\end{cases}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Utilize the Reality of the Potential

Because the potential $V_a(x)$ is a real function, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is a strictly real differential equation. This implies that the complex conjugate of any valid solution is also a solution with the same energy $E$. 

Conjugating $\psi_L(x)$, we obtain another valid solution:
$$
\psi_L^*(x) = \begin{cases}
e^{-ik(x+a)} + N^* e^{ik(x+a)}, & x < -a \\
S^* e^{-ik(x-a)}, & x > a
\end{cases}
$$
Since $S \neq 0$ (assuming finite barriers), $\psi_L(x)$ and $\psi_L^*(x)$ are linearly independent and thus form a complete basis of solutions for the second-order ODE at energy $E$.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Express $\psi_R(x)$ in terms of $\psi_L(x)$ and $\psi_L^*(x)$

We can write $\psi_R(x)$ as a linear combination of our two independent solutions:
$$
\psi_R(x) = A \psi_L(x) + B \psi_L^*(x)
$$
where $A$ and $B$ are complex constants.

We match the asymptotic forms in the region $x > a$ to determine $A$ and $B$. Substituting the known right-side forms into the linear combination:
$$
A\left(S e^{ik(x-a)}\right) + B\left(S^* e^{-ik(x-a)}\right) = e^{-ik(x-a)} + N' e^{ik(x-a)}
$$
Comparing the coefficients of the left-going wave $e^{-ik(x-a)}$ and the right-going wave $e^{ik(x-a)}$, we find:
$$
B S^* = 1 \implies B = \frac{1}{S^*}
$$
$$
A S = N' \implies A = \frac{N'}{S}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Match the Asymptotic Forms for $x < -a$

Now we use the coefficients $A$ and $B$ to evaluate the linear combination in the region $x < -a$. 
Substituting $\psi_L$ and $\psi_L^*$ into the linear combination and equating to the known left-side form of $\psi_R$:
$$
A\left(e^{ik(x+a)} + N e^{-ik(x+a)}\right) + B\left(e^{-ik(x+a)} + N^* e^{ik(x+a)}\right) = S' e^{-ik(x+a)}
$$
Gathering the coefficients of the right-going wave $e^{ik(x+a)}$ and the left-going wave $e^{-ik(x+a)}$:
$$
(A + B N^*) e^{ik(x+a)} + (A N + B) e^{-ik(x+a)} = S' e^{-ik(x+a)}
$$
For the coefficient of $e^{ik(x+a)}$ (which represents an incoming wave from the left, and thus must be zero for right-incidence):
$$
A + B N^* = 0
$$
Substituting our expressions for $A$ and $B$:
$$
\frac{N'}{S} + \frac{N^*}{S^*} = 0 \implies N' = -N^* \frac{S}{S^*}
$$
For the coefficient of $e^{-ik(x+a)}$ (which represents the transmitted wave):
$$
A N + B = S'
$$
Substitute $B = \frac{1}{S^*}$ and $A = -\frac{N^*}{S^*}$ (derived from the previous step):
$$
\left(-\frac{N^*}{S^*}\right) N + \frac{1}{S^*} = S' \implies S' = \frac{1 - |N|^2}{S^*}
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 5: Apply Probability Current Conservation

To simplify the expression for $S'$, we invoke the conservation of probability current. For the left-incident state $\psi_L(x)$, the incoming probability flux must equal the sum of the reflected and transmitted fluxes:
$$
1 = |N|^2 + |S|^2 \implies 1 - |N|^2 = |S|^2
$$
Substituting this conservation law into our equation for $S'$:
$$
S' = \frac{|S|^2}{S^*} = \frac{S S^*}{S^*} = S
$$
Thus, the transmission amplitude is identical regardless of the direction of the incident wave.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{S' = S} \quad \text{and} \quad \boxed{N' = -N^* \frac{S}{S^*}}
$$

---

## Discussion

The equality $S' = S$ proves that the transmission probability $|S'|^2 = |S|^2$ is perfectly independent of the direction of incidence, and additionally, the phase shift acquired by the wave during transmission is invariant under the choice of left or right incidence.

The relation $N' = -N^* \frac{S}{S^*}$ shows that the reflection probability is also symmetric ($|N'|^2 = |N|^2$), but the phase of the reflected wave is generally dependent on the direction of incidence. Specifically, the relationship constrains the relative phases of the scattering matrix elements. For a strictly symmetric potential $V(x) = V(-x)$, the system is invariant under parity transformations, yielding $N' = N$. The equations we've derived here are the cornerstone of the generalized unitarity of the S-matrix in 1D quantum scattering.

## Related Resources

- **Typical method used**: Linear superposition of a full set of basis states derived from parity invariance or explicit wave matching, combined with S-matrix unitarity.
- **Alternative approaches**: The transfer matrix (M-matrix) formalism provides an exceptionally elegant alternative proof. Using $\begin{pmatrix} C_{\text{out}} \\ C_{\text{in}} \end{pmatrix}_R = M \begin{pmatrix} C_{\text{in}} \\ C_{\text{out}} \end{pmatrix}_L$, time-reversal symmetry imposes specific conditions on $M$ which immediately yields $S_{12} = S_{21}$.
