# Problem 4.12

**Source**: Chapter 4, Problem 12

## Problem Statement

12. The effective mass $m^*$ of an electron in a solid with energy $E(k)$ is defined in one dimension by
$$
\frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2} \frac{\dd^2 E}{\dd k^2}.
$$
Calculate $m^*$, in the Kronig-Penney model with delta functions, at the bottom and at the top of the lowest energy band. Explain the physical significance of the results.

---

## Restatement

An electron in a one-dimensional periodic delta-function potential (the Kronig-Penney model), $V(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty v_0 \delta(x-na)$, has an energy dispersion $E(k)$ for its Bloch waves. The effective mass $m^*$ is defined by
$$ 
\frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2} \frac{\dd^2 E}{\dd k^2} 
$$
Calculate $m^*$ at the bottom ($k=0$) and top ($k=\pi/a$) of the lowest energy band in terms of the model parameters. Discuss the physical significance of the results. Let $P = \frac{m v_0 a}{\hbar^2}$ denote the dimensionless strength of the potential.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Kronig-Penney Dispersion Relation**: The energy $E = \frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{2m}$ is related to the Bloch wavevector $k$ by the transcendental equation:
$$ \cos ka = \cos qa + P \frac{\sin qa}{qa} $$
where $P = \frac{mv_0a}{\hbar^2}$.
- **Effective Mass**: The effective mass $m^*$ quantifies how an electron responds to external forces in a crystal lattice, incorporating the effect of the periodic potential.

---

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — The bottom of the lowest band corresponds to $k=0$, and the top of the lowest band corresponds to $k=\pi/a$.
> **Hint 2** — Instead of trying to solve for $E(k)$ explicitly, use implicit differentiation on the dispersion relation to find $\dd q/\dd k$ and $\dd^2 q/\dd k^2$.
> **Hint 3** — Express the derivatives $\dd E/\dd k$ and $\dd^2 E/\dd k^2$ in terms of $q$, $\dd q/\dd k$, and $\dd^2 q/\dd k^2$. Note that at the band edges, the first derivative $\dd q/\dd k$ vanishes.

---

## Solution

### Setup

The energy of the electron is given by $E = \frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{2m}$. The effective mass definition is evaluated using the chain rule:
$$ \frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2} \frac{\dd^2 E}{\dd k^2} = \frac{1}{m} \frac{\dd}{\dd k} \left( q \frac{\dd q}{\dd k} \right) = \frac{1}{m} \left[ \left( \frac{\dd q}{\dd k} \right)^2 + q \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} \right] $$
The wavevectors $k$ and $q$ are related by the Kronig-Penney condition:
$$ \cos ka = f(q) \equiv \cos qa + P \frac{\sin qa}{qa} $$
where $P = \frac{mv_0a}{\hbar^2}$. To find the effective mass, we differentiate this relation implicitly with respect to $k$:
$$ -a \sin ka = f'(q) \frac{\dd q}{\dd k} $$
Differentiating a second time yields:
$$ -a^2 \cos ka = f''(q) \left( \frac{\dd q}{\dd k} \right)^2 + f'(q) \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} $$

### Step 1: Bottom of the lowest band ($k=0$)

At the bottom of the lowest band, $k = 0$, which gives $\cos ka = 1$ and $\sin ka = 0$.
The corresponding value $q_0$ is the solution to $f(q_0) = 1$, or $\cos q_0 a + P \frac{\sin q_0 a}{q_0 a} = 1$. Let $x_0 = q_0 a$.
Since $\sin(0) = 0$, the first derivative relation gives $f'(q_0) \frac{\dd q}{\dd k} = 0$. Assuming $f'(q_0) \neq 0$, we have $\frac{\dd q}{\dd k} = 0$.
The second derivative relation evaluated at $k=0$ then becomes:
$$ -a^2 = f'(q_0) \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} \implies \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} = -\frac{a^2}{f'(q_0)} $$
The first derivative $f'(q)$ is:
$$ f'(q) = \frac{\dd}{\dd q} \left( \cos qa + P \frac{\sin qa}{qa} \right) = -a \sin qa + P \frac{qa \cos qa - \sin qa}{q^2 a} $$
Letting $x_0 = q_0 a$, we write this as:
$$ f'(q_0) = \frac{a}{x_0} \left( -x_0 \sin x_0 + P \cos x_0 - P \frac{\sin x_0}{x_0} \right) $$
From the energy condition $f(q_0) = 1$, we can isolate $P = \frac{x_0(1 - \cos x_0)}{\sin x_0}$. Substituting this into $f'(q_0)$:
$$ f'(q_0) = \frac{a}{x_0} \left( -x_0 \sin x_0 + \frac{x_0(1 - \cos x_0)}{\sin x_0} \cos x_0 - (1 - \cos x_0) \right) $$
Pulling out a common denominator of $\sin x_0$:
$$ f'(q_0) = \frac{a}{x_0 \sin x_0} \left( -x_0 \sin^2 x_0 + x_0 \cos x_0 - x_0 \cos^2 x_0 - \sin x_0(1 - \cos x_0) \right) $$
Since $-x_0 \sin^2 x_0 - x_0 \cos^2 x_0 = -x_0$, the terms in the parenthesis become:
$$ -x_0(1 - \cos x_0) - \sin x_0(1 - \cos x_0) = -(1 - \cos x_0)(x_0 + \sin x_0) $$
Thus, we have:
$$ f'(q_0) = -\frac{a}{x_0 \sin x_0} (1 - \cos x_0)(x_0 + \sin x_0) $$
Substituting this back into $\frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2}$:
$$ \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} = \frac{a x_0 \sin x_0}{(1 - \cos x_0)(x_0 + \sin x_0)} $$
Finally, evaluating the effective mass at $k=0$ (where $\frac{\dd q}{\dd k} = 0$):
$$ \frac{1}{m^*_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{1}{m} \left( q_0 \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} \right) = \frac{1}{m} \left( \frac{x_0}{a} \right) \left[ \frac{a x_0 \sin x_0}{(1 - \cos x_0)(x_0 + \sin x_0)} \right] = \frac{1}{m} \frac{x_0^2 \sin x_0}{(1 - \cos x_0)(x_0 + \sin x_0)} $$
Using the half-angle identity $\frac{\sin x_0}{1 - \cos x_0} = \cot(x_0/2)$:
$$ m^*_{\text{bottom}} = m \frac{x_0 + \sin x_0}{x_0^2} \tan(x_0/2) $$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 2: Top of the lowest band ($k=\pi/a$)

At the top of the lowest band, $k = \pi/a$, which implies $\cos ka = -1$ and $\sin ka = 0$.
The corresponding value $q_1$ is the solution to $f(q_1) = -1$. By inspection of $f(q_1) = \cos q_1 a + P \frac{\sin q_1 a}{q_1 a} = -1$, this occurs precisely when $q_1 a = \pi$, since $\cos \pi = -1$ and $\sin \pi = 0$.
Thus $q_1 = \pi/a$.
Again, $\sin ka = 0 \implies \frac{\dd q}{\dd k} = 0$.
The second derivative relation at $k=\pi/a$ becomes:
$$ -a^2(-1) = f'(q_1) \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} \implies \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} = \frac{a^2}{f'(\pi/a)} $$
We evaluate the first derivative $f'(q)$ at $qa = \pi$:
$$ f'(\pi/a) = -a \sin \pi + P \frac{\pi \cos \pi - \sin \pi}{\pi^2/a} = P \frac{-\pi}{\pi^2/a} = -P \frac{a}{\pi} $$
Thus, solving for $\frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2}$:
$$ \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} = \frac{a^2}{-P a / \pi} = -\frac{\pi a}{P} $$
The effective mass at $k=\pi/a$ is then:
$$ \frac{1}{m^*_{\text{top}}} = \frac{1}{m} \left( q_1 \frac{\dd^2 q}{\dd k^2} \right) = \frac{1}{m} \left( \frac{\pi}{a} \right) \left( -\frac{\pi a}{P} \right) = -\frac{\pi^2}{m P} $$
Inverting this gives:
$$ m^*_{\text{top}} = -m \frac{P}{\pi^2} $$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{ m^*_{\text{bottom}} = m \frac{x_0 + \sin x_0}{x_0^2} \tan(x_0/2) \quad \text{where } \cos x_0 + P\frac{\sin x_0}{x_0} = 1 }
$$
$$
\boxed{ m^*_{\text{top}} = -m \frac{P}{\pi^2} }
$$

## Discussion

**Physical Significance:**
1. At the **bottom of the band**, the effective mass is positive and greater than the bare mass $m$. The electron behaves like a regular particle, but its acceleration is hindered by Bragg reflections from the periodic lattice. As the lattice potential vanishes ($P \to 0$), $x_0 \to 0$ and we recover $m^*_{\text{bottom}} \to m$ (the free particle limit). In the strong potential limit ($P \to \infty$), the electron becomes heavily localized, the energy band becomes extremely narrow, and $m^*_{\text{bottom}} \to \infty$.
2. At the **top of the band**, the effective mass is *negative*. This means that if an external force is applied, the electron will accelerate in the *opposite* direction to the force. This counter-intuitive behavior occurs because strong Bragg reflections near the Brillouin zone boundary dominate over the external force. In semiconductors, nearly full bands filled with negative effective mass electrons are mathematically and physically equivalent to sparsely populated bands of positively charged particles with a *positive* effective mass, commonly known as **holes**.
