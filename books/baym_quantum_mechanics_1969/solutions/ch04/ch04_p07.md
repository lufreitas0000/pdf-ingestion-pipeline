# Problem 4.7

**Source**: Chapter 4, Problem 7

## Problem Statement

7. (a) Show that the energy levels of a double square well
$$
V_S(x) = \begin{cases}
\infty, & |x| > b \\
0, & a < |x| < b \\
\infty, & |x| < a
\end{cases}
$$
are doubly degenerate.
(b) Now suppose that the barrier between $-a$ and $a$ is very high, but finite. Assume that the potential between $-a$ and $a$ is symmetric about the origin. There is now the possibility of tunneling from one well to the other, and this possibility has the effect of splitting the degeneracy of the double well in part (a). Let $E_0$ be an energy level of the well in part (a), and assume that $E_0$ is reasonably less than the barrier height. Assume that in the neighborhood of $E_0$ the reflection amplitude of the barrier at $-a$ is of the form $-e^{2i\delta(E)}$ where $\delta(E)$ is real, positive, and $\ll 1$. (Can $\delta(E)$ be otherwise under these conditions?) Also assume $S(E)$ to be of the form $iJ(E)$ where $J$ is small and positive. (Must the leading term in $S(E)$ as the barrier height becomes infinite be of this form?)
Show that to lowest order in $\delta$ and $J$ the well with the finite barrier has two levels $E$ corresponding to each degenerate level of the double square well in (a), given by
$$
E_\pm = E_0 - \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left[ \delta(E_0) \mp \frac{J(E_0)}{2} \right].
$$
What are the parities of the split levels?
(c) If $\delta(E)$ is positive, as it generally must be, then one level of the double well with tunneling is lower in energy than $E_0$. This effect provides a qualitative explanation of the "one electron bond" between two ions, e.g., the bond in $\text{H}_2^+$. The ions, when far apart, correspond to the double square well of part (a). When they are close together there is a possibility of an electron tunneling from one ion to the other. Due to the tunneling splitting the electron, and hence the two ions plus electron, can have a lower energy when the two ions are close together; hence they bind. Estimate the binding energy of two ions due to this mechanism.

---

## Restatement

Consider a particle of mass $m$ in a symmetric double square well potential:
$$
V_S(x) = \begin{cases}
\infty, & |x| > b \\
0, & a < |x| < b \\
V_B(x), & |x| < a
\end{cases}
$$
(a) For an infinite barrier $V_B(x) = \infty$, show that all energy levels are doubly degenerate.
(b) For a finite, very high symmetric barrier $V_B(x) = V_B(-x)$, tunneling splits the degeneracy. For an energy $E \approx E_0$ (where $E_0$ is an unperturbed level), the barrier's reflection amplitude is $R = -e^{2i\delta(E)}$ (with $0 < \delta \ll 1$) and transmission amplitude is $S = iJ(E)$ (with $0 < J \ll 1$). Show that the split levels are:
$$
E_\pm = E_0 - \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left[ \delta(E_0) \mp \frac{J(E_0)}{2} \right]
$$
Determine the parities of the states corresponding to $E_\pm$. Address whether $\delta > 0$ is strictly required, and whether $J$ must intrinsically be positive.
(c) Assuming $\delta > 0$, estimate the binding energy between two ions (modeled as the wells) due to this tunneling mechanism.

---

## Prerequisites

- **Infinite Square Well**: The energy levels for a well of width $L$ are $E_n = \frac{\pi^2 \hbar^2 n^2}{2m L^2}$, corresponding to unperturbed momentum $p_0 = \hbar k_0 = \frac{n \pi \hbar}{L}$.
- **Scattering Matrix (1D)**: The outgoing wave amplitudes $(O_L, O_R)$ from a symmetric barrier are related to the incoming wave amplitudes $(I_L, I_R)$ via the reflection $R$ and transmission $S$ amplitudes: $O_L = R I_L + S I_R$ and $O_R = S I_L + R I_R$.
- **Momentum Expansion**: For a small energy shift $\Delta E$, the wave vector $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ expands as $k \approx k_0 + \frac{m}{\hbar^2 k_0} \Delta E = k_0 + \frac{m}{p_0 \hbar} \Delta E$.

## Hints

> **Hint 1** — For part (b), write down the general solutions in the zero-potential regions ($-b < x < -a$ and $a < x < b$) as standing waves that strictly vanish at the outer rigid boundaries $x = \pm b$.

> **Hint 2** — Decompose these standing waves into left-moving and right-moving components at the barrier boundaries $x = \pm a$. Identify which components represent waves incident on the barrier and which represent outgoing waves.

> **Hint 3** — Relate the incoming and outgoing components using the given $R = -e^{2i\delta}$ and $S = iJ$. This will yield a system of two equations. Setting the determinant to zero will give the quantization condition.

> **Hint 4** — Expand the exponent $e^{-2ik(b-a)}$ to first order in $\Delta E = E - E_0$ around the unperturbed momentum $k_0$, and match terms with the expanded scattering amplitudes to solve for $\Delta E$.

> **Hint 5** — For the parenthetical questions, think about how barrier penetration increases the "effective" width of a well (affecting $\delta$), and compute $S(E)$ for a simple delta-function or square barrier to check the natural sign of $J$.

## Solution

### Step 1: Infinite Barrier Degeneracy (Part a)

When $V_B(x) = \infty$ for $|x| < a$, the system decomposes into two completely isolated infinite square wells, each of width $L = b - a$. 
The left well occupies $-b < x < -a$, and the right well occupies $a < x < b$. 
The energy eigenstates for the left well are:
$$
\psi_L^{(n)}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left[ \frac{n\pi(x+b)}{L} \right], \quad x \in (-b, -a)
$$
The eigenstates for the right well are:
$$
\psi_R^{(n)}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left[ \frac{n\pi(b-x)}{L} \right], \quad x \in (a, b)
$$
Both sets of states share the exact same discrete energy spectrum:
$$
E_n = \frac{n^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2}{2m L^2}
$$
Because the wells are completely isolated by the infinite barrier, any linear combination $\alpha \psi_L^{(n)} + \beta \psi_R^{(n)}$ is a valid eigenstate with energy $E_n$. Therefore, every energy level is doubly degenerate.

### Step 2: Formulating the Scattering Conditions (Part b)

When the barrier is finite, particles can tunnel between the wells. In the regions $a < |x| < b$, the potential is still zero, so the solutions must be linear combinations of plane waves $e^{\pm ikx}$ that vanish at the outer walls $x = \pm b$.
We write the wavefunctions in the left and right wells as:
$$
\psi_L(x) = \frac{B}{2i} \left[ e^{ik(x+b)} - e^{-ik(x+b)} \right], \quad x \in (-b, -a)
$$
$$
\psi_R(x) = \frac{A}{2i} \left[ e^{ik(b-x)} - e^{-ik(b-x)} \right], \quad x \in (a, b)
$$
To apply the barrier's scattering matrix, we need the wave components exactly at the barrier boundaries $x = -a$ and $x = a$. Let $L = b-a$.
In the left well, rearranging $\psi_L$ in terms of distance from the barrier ($x+a$):
$$
\psi_L(x) = \frac{B}{2i} \left[ e^{ikL} e^{ik(x+a)} - e^{-ikL} e^{-ik(x+a)} \right]
$$
Here, $e^{ik(x+a)}$ represents the wave traveling to the right (incident on the barrier from the left), and $e^{-ik(x+a)}$ is traveling to the left (outgoing from the barrier).
In the right well, rearranging $\psi_R$ in terms of distance from the barrier ($x-a$):
$$
\psi_R(x) = \frac{A}{2i} \left[ e^{ikL} e^{-ik(x-a)} - e^{-ikL} e^{ik(x-a)} \right]
$$
Here, $e^{-ik(x-a)}$ travels to the left (incident on the barrier from the right), and $e^{ik(x-a)}$ travels to the right (outgoing from the barrier).

We define the incoming amplitudes $I$ and outgoing amplitudes $O$:
$$
I_L = \frac{B}{2i} e^{ikL}, \quad O_L = -\frac{B}{2i} e^{-ikL} \quad (\text{at } x = -a)
$$
$$
I_R = \frac{A}{2i} e^{ikL}, \quad O_R = -\frac{A}{2i} e^{-ikL} \quad (\text{at } x = a)
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 3: Applying the Scattering Matrix

The barrier mixes the incoming waves to produce the outgoing waves via the reflection amplitude $R = -e^{2i\delta}$ and the transmission amplitude $S = iJ$:
$$
O_L = R I_L + S I_R
$$
$$
O_R = S I_L + R I_R
$$
Substituting the expressions from Step 2:
$$
-\frac{B}{2i} e^{-ikL} = -e^{2i\delta} \frac{B}{2i} e^{ikL} + iJ \frac{A}{2i} e^{ikL} \implies B e^{-ikL} = B e^{i(kL + 2\delta)} - iJ A e^{ikL}
$$
$$
-\frac{A}{2i} e^{-ikL} = iJ \frac{B}{2i} e^{ikL} - e^{2i\delta} \frac{A}{2i} e^{ikL} \implies A e^{-ikL} = -iJ B e^{ikL} + A e^{i(kL + 2\delta)}
$$
Multiplying both equations by $e^{-ikL}$ yields a symmetric eigenvalue problem:
$$
B(e^{-2ikL} - e^{2i\delta}) = -i J A
$$
$$
A(e^{-2ikL} - e^{2i\delta}) = -i J B
$$
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 4: Solving for the Split Energy Levels

Dividing the two equations gives $A^2 = B^2$, meaning $B = \pm A$.
- **Even Parity State ($B = A$)**: 
Substituting $B = A$ into the equations gives:
$$
e^{-2ikL} - e^{2i\delta} = -iJ \implies e^{-2ikL} = e^{2i\delta} - iJ
$$
When $B = A$, one can easily check that $\psi_L(-x) = \psi_R(x)$, so the overall wavefunction satisfies $\psi(-x) = \psi(x)$, which is even parity.

- **Odd Parity State ($B = -A$)**:
Substituting $B = -A$ gives:
$$
e^{-2ikL} - e^{2i\delta} = iJ \implies e^{-2ikL} = e^{2i\delta} + iJ
$$
When $B = -A$, we find $\psi_L(-x) = -\psi_R(x)$, which corresponds to $\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)$, which is odd parity.

Now, we solve for the energy shift. Let $E = E_0 + \Delta E$. The unperturbed momentum satisfies $k_0 L = n\pi$.
The momentum $k$ shifts by $\Delta k \approx \frac{\dd k}{\dd E}\Delta E = \frac{m}{\hbar^2 k_0}\Delta E = \frac{m}{p_0\hbar}\Delta E$.
Expanding the exponential to first order in $\Delta E$:
$$
e^{-2ikL} = e^{-2i(k_0 + \Delta k)L} = e^{-2in\pi} e^{-2i\Delta k L} = 1 - 2i \frac{mL}{p_0\hbar} \Delta E
$$
Similarly, expanding $e^{2i\delta} \approx 1 + 2i\delta$.
The quantization conditions $e^{-2ikL} = e^{2i\delta} \mp iJ$ become:
$$
1 - 2i \frac{mL}{p_0\hbar} \Delta E_{\text{even}/\text{odd}} = 1 + 2i\delta \mp iJ
$$
Solving for $\Delta E$:
$$
-2i \frac{mL}{p_0\hbar} \Delta E_{\text{even}/\text{odd}} = 2i\delta \mp iJ \implies \Delta E_{\text{even}/\text{odd}} = -\frac{p_0\hbar}{mL} \left( \delta \mp \frac{J}{2} \right)
$$
Writing this in the form requested by the problem:
$$
E_\pm = E_0 - \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left[ \delta(E_0) \mp \frac{J(E_0)}{2} \right]
$$
Comparing our results, $E_+$ corresponds to $\Delta E = -\frac{p_0\hbar}{mL} (\delta - J/2)$, which is the **even parity** state. $E_-$ corresponds to $\Delta E = -\frac{p_0\hbar}{mL} (\delta + J/2)$, which is the **odd parity** state.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 5: Nature of $\delta$ and $J$ (Parenthetical Questions)

1. **Can $\delta(E)$ be otherwise?** No, for a repulsive barrier, $\delta$ must be positive. An infinite wall gives $\delta = 0$. A finite repulsive barrier allows the wavefunction to exponentially decay into the classically forbidden region. This shifts the effective boundary of the well slightly outwards, increasing the effective width of the well. A wider well means a longer wavelength and a lower kinetic energy, corresponding to a downward energy shift ($\Delta E \propto -\delta$), which requires $\delta > 0$.
2. **Must $J$ be positive?** No. While the problem posits $J > 0$ for the sake of the derived expression, calculating the exact transmission amplitude for a standard repulsive square or delta-function barrier yields $S \approx -i \times (\text{positive real})$, meaning physically $J$ is generally **negative**. If $J < 0$, then the even parity state $E_+$ would have a *lower* energy than the odd parity state $E_-$, which aligns perfectly with the fundamental quantum mechanical theorem that the ground state is nodeless and uniquely even.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Step 6: The "One Electron Bond" Binding Energy (Part c)

When two distant ions are brought close to distance $2a$, the infinite barrier becomes a finite barrier, and the degenerate single-ion levels $E_0$ split into $E_+$ and $E_-$.
Assuming $\delta > 0$ and using the physically accurate negative $J$ (so $-J = |J|$), both levels are shifted downwards by the barrier penetration, and further split by tunneling:
$$
E_{\text{even/odd}} = E_0 - \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left( \delta \pm \frac{|J|}{2} \right)
$$
The electron will spontaneously relax into the lowest available state, which is the even parity state.
The total energy of the system is lowered relative to the separated ions. This energy reduction is the binding energy of the one-electron bond:
$$
E_{\text{binding}} = E_0 - E_{\text{even}} \approx \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left( \delta(E_0) + \frac{|J(E_0)|}{2} \right)
$$
This lowering of the electron's kinetic energy acts as an attractive potential between the two ions, creating a stable chemical bond.
<!-- CONFIDENT -->

### Result

$$
\boxed{E_\pm = E_0 - \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left[ \delta(E_0) \mp \frac{J(E_0)}{2} \right]}
$$
- $E_+$ has **even** parity.
- $E_-$ has **odd** parity.
- Binding energy $\approx \frac{p_0\hbar}{m(b-a)} \left( \delta(E_0) + \frac{|J(E_0)|}{2} \right)$.

## Discussion

The problem elegantly illustrates how tunneling breaks degeneracy, leading to molecular bonding. The downward shift proportional to $\delta(E_0)$ is a single-well effect representing the "softening" of the hard wall, which allows the wavefunction to spread out more and lowers the kinetic energy. The $\mp J(E_0)/2$ term is the true interaction term resulting from the overlap of the wavefunctions through the barrier, representing the frequency of tunneling between the left and right wells. The bonding state is always symmetric (even parity) without nodes in the barrier, minimizing the curvature and thus the kinetic energy.

## Notes

The problem's prompt asking whether $J$ is positive ("Must the leading term in $S(E)$ as the barrier height becomes infinite be of this form?") is an excellent pedagogical trap. Following the math strictly yields $E_+$ as the even state, and if $J$ were positive, the odd state would be the ground state. Proving $J$ is actually negative for typical barriers ensures physical consistency.
